# Griffin et al. 2025

## Verbatim abstract

> "Flats ecosystems are dynamic, shallow, nearshore marine environments that are interconnected and provide immense ecological and socio-economic benefits. These habitats support a diversity of fish populations and various fisheries, yet they are increasingly threatened by anthropogenic stressors, including overfishing, habitat degradation, coastal development, and the cascading effects of climate change. Effective habitat management and restoration are essential but are often missing for flats ecosystems. Despite navigating a landscape of imperfect knowledge for these systems, decisive action and implementation of habitat protection and restoration is currently needed through policy and practice. We present a comprehensive set of 10 strategic guiding principles necessary for integrating habitat management and restoration for the conservation of interconnected flat ecosystems. These principles include calls for comprehensive ecosystem-based management, integrating adaptive strategies that leverage diverse partnerships, scientific research, legislative initiatives, and local and traditional ecological knowledge. Drawing on successes in other environmental management realms, we emphasize the importance of evidence-informed approaches to address the complexities and uncertainties of flats ecosystems. These guiding principles aim to advance flats habitat management and restoration, promoting ecological integrity and strengthening the socio-economic resilience of these important marine environments." (Abstract, p. 288 (PDF p. 2))

### Cited comparison values

#### Habitat extent and loss

- Under 0.5% of ocean surface, 50% of carbon burial (Duarte et al. 2005): "Although covering less than half a percent of the ocean surface, flats and associated habitats, such as seagrasses, salt marshes, macroalgae, and mangroves, are responsible for 50% of carbon burial in marine sediments (Duarte et al., 2005)" (Introduction, p. 288 (PDF p. 2))
- 68% of tidal flats under human pressure, 2014 to 2016 (Hill et al. 2021): "Recent estimates (2014–2016) indicate that 68% of tidal flats experience moderate to very high levels of human pressure, ranging from infilling and coastal hardening, vegetation loss, reduced sediment flow, and increased nutrient loads (Hill et al., 2021)." (Introduction, p. 288 (PDF p. 2))
- 16% tidal flat loss, 1984 to 2016 (Murray et al. 2019): "over the past few decades, tidal flats in North America, East Asia, and the Middle East have experienced a substantial decline, with approximately 16% of these critical habitats disappearing between 1984 and 2016 (Murray et al., 2019)" (Introduction, p. 288 (PDF p. 2))
- 18% of mangrove, seagrass, reef interaction zones protected (R. R. Carlson et al. 2021): "in a global analysis of where mangrove, seagrass, and reef communities co-occurred, R. R. Carlson et al. (2021) estimated only 18% of these interaction zones were covered by protected areas" (Insert habitat protection and restoration into development and planning processes, p. 290 (PDF p. 4))
- 16.6% of land, 7.7% of ocean protected (Bingham et al. 2021): "habitat protection remains more commonly used in terrestrial ecosystems, where 16.6% of land and inland water ecosystems is protected but only 7.7% of oceans (Bingham et al., 2021)" (Synthesis and Conclusion, p. 294 (PDF p. 8))
- 55% of marine spatial plans address land-sea integration (Ehler 2021): "with only 55% of MSP claiming to address land–sea integration in their plans (Ehler, 2021), MSP often lacks the integration of terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecosystems' connectivity" (Insert habitat protection and restoration into development and planning processes, p. 290 (PDF p. 4))
- United Nations decade (United Nations 2019): "the United Nations declaring the years 2021 to 2030 as the 'Decade on Ecosystem Restoration' (United Nations, 2019)" (Synthesis and Conclusion, p. 294 (PDF p. 8))

#### Fishery value and trends

- US$474 million per year, 2012 (M. - Two-fold rise in bonefish catch rates since 2015 (Boucek et al. 2023): "Boucek et al. (Introduction, p. 288 (PDF p. 2))
- 55 co-occurring species under the Common Snook umbrella (Wilson et al. 2023): "protecting their nursery habitats, including mangrove creeks and wetlands, not only benefits snook but also supports the conservation of 55 other co-occurring species (Wilson et al., 2023)" (Embrace flats fishes as flagship and umbrella species, p. 289 (PDF p. 3))
- Median 70% of permit prey from seagrass (Brownscombe et al. 2022b): "Brownscombe et al. - Tarpon migration distance (Griffin et al. 2023a; Luo et al. 2020): "adult Atlantic Tarpon undertake extensive seasonal migrations, sometimes thousands of kilometers, exposing them to a variety of habitat and fisheries-related stressors (Griffin et al., 2023a; Luo et al., 2020)" (Consider connectivity, p. 290 (PDF p. 4))

- 77 projects, 7,000 acres, Columbia River estuary (Littles et al. 2022): "within the Columbia River estuary, a combined 77 restoration projects were iteratively implemented to improve juvenile salmonid habitats resulting in 7,000 acres of floodplain habitat restored (Littles et al., 2022)" (Learn from habitat management and restoration in other realms, p. 291 (PDF p. 5))
- Three United States case studies (DeAngelis et al. 2020): "DeAngelis et al. - Stream restoration evidence base (Foote et al. 2020; Whiteway et al. 2010): "there are hundreds of studies on stream restoration with endpoints of fish biomass and abundance that have been conducted enabling meta-analysis (Foote et al., 2020; Whiteway et al., 2010)" (Build the evidence base, p. 291 (PDF p. 5))

#### Catch-and-release and predation

No numeric post-release survival or predation rates are given; the paper cites the studies qualitatively.

- Variable post-release predation (Cooke & Philipp 2004; Danylchuk et al. 2007a, 2007b; Lennox et al. 2017; Moxham et al. 2019): "Post-release predation rates of bonefish after recreational angling events are highly variable (Cooke & Philipp, 2004; Danylchuk et al., 2007a, 2007b; Lennox et al., 2017; Moxham et al., 2019) and in some instances, may be more influenced by existing predator burden in the angling habitat than angler handling practices (Lennox et al., 2017), like air exposure (Cooke & Philipp, 2004; Lennox et al., 2017)." (Think and act at the systems level, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Deeper water post-release (Danylchuk et al. 2007b; Brownscombe et al. 2019c): "Bonefish have been observed using deeper water post-release (Danylchuk et al., 2007b), which may be a strategy to avoid visual predators like Great Barracuda Sphyraena barracuda that occupy the flats (Brownscombe et al., 2019c)." (Think and act at the systems level, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Hammerhead depredation of tarpon (Casselberry et al. 2024): "this conflict between flats species and protected predators is especially true for Atlantic Tarpon, which are regularly depredated by Great Hammerhead Sphyrna mokarran—a protected species in Florida state waters—in the Florida Keys recreational fishery (Casselberry et al., 2024)" (Think and act at the systems level, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Predator density across the seascape (Griffin et al. 2022): "Predator density and movements will vary across the seascape, influencing natural mortality as well as the potential for fishery induced mortality for recreationally targeted flats species (Griffin et al., 2022)." (Think and act at the systems level, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Predation around restored oyster reefs (Duncan et al. 2019): "in Queensland, Australia, Duncan et al. (2019) reported that predation rates were highest when restored oyster reefs were surrounded by non-vegetated seafloor far from seagrass and mangrove habitats" (Think and act at the systems level, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Catch-and-release inside no-take areas (Cooke et al. 2006): "Designating catch-and-release species as conservation surrogates can support conservation goals, particularly in alignment with no-take areas, provided that mortality, whether through depredation or post-release, remains low and bycatch is minimized (Cooke et al., 2006)." (Synthesis and Conclusion, p. 294 (PDF p. 8))

## Methods worth reusing

The paper has no methods section. The sentences below describe approaches the authors point to for flats restoration work.

- Monitoring tools: "Techniques, such as electronic tagging (Brownscombe et al., 2022a; Gahagan & Bailey, 2020; Lapointe et al., 2013; Piczak et al., 2024) and the integration of ecological knowledge (Mamun, 2010; Uprety et al., 2012), have shown promising success and could offer valuable insights if broadly applied to the flats ecosystems." (Build the evidence base, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Isotopes and eDNA: "stable isotope analysis (Loch & Cook, 2023; Wozniak et al., 2006) and environmental DNA studies (Capurso et al., 2023; Wee et al., 2023) are emerging as powerful tools for establishing ecological connections, monitoring biodiversity, and establishing habitat linkages" (Build the evidence base, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Experimental design: "There is a need for more experimental approaches to flats and coastal restoration, incorporating appropriate reference sites, replication, and long-term monitoring (Cooke et al., 2019)." (Build the evidence base, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Standard monitoring framework: "A standardized marine restoration monitoring framework is necessary to ensure that detailed reporting contributes to the evidence base (Eger et al., 2022)." (Build the evidence base, pp. 291 to 292 (PDF pp. 5 to 6))
- Adaptive management: "adaptive management playing an important role, whereby new information is iteratively incorporated into decision making and management to reduce uncertainty (Williams & Brown, 2014)" (Learn from habitat management and restoration in other realms, p. 291 (PDF p. 5))
- Partner timing: "Partnership engagement is most effective when partners are included through each step of the process (i.e., management design, implementation, and evaluation; G. Smith, 2018). Therefore, partners should be brought in early on when projects are being considered." (Engage in effective partnerships, p. 291 (PDF p. 5))
- Sediment design rule: "Restoration designs should emulate natural flats by encouraging silt accumulation where the wave energy is low, slope is gentle, and hydrological connectivity is maintained (Ganju, 2019; Lee et al., 1998; Li et al., 2021)." (Emulate nature, p. 293 (PDF p. 7))
- Seagrass prop-scar repair: "in seagrass meadows, placing bird stakes over prop scars accelerates recovery by enhancing nutrient fluxes from wild bird feces (Kenworthy et al., 2018)" (Emulate nature, p. 293 (PDF p. 7))
- Mangrove planting siting: "This includes considering sea level rise projections and extreme storm disturbance probabilities for guiding planting locations and employing additional tactics, such as breakwaters, to enhance survival and growth (Fillyaw et al., 2021)." (Future-proof restoration, p. 293 (PDF p. 7))
- Spatial data integration: "in the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, integrating spatial data on human activity, e.g., fishing effort (Black et al., 2015) and recreational boating (Anderson, 2022; Boucek et al., 2024), has been and will continue to be important for revising spatial management strategies" (Insert habitat protection and restoration into development and planning processes, p. 290 (PDF p. 4))

## Management statements

- Flagship designation: "Like Common Snook, recreational flats species should be recognized as flagship species." (Embrace flats fishes as flagship and umbrella species, p. 289 (PDF p. 3))
- Umbrella habitats: "Thus, focusing on the protection of mangrove and seagrass habitats for permit, would, in turn, support the habitats that serve as nurseries for both artisanal and commercial fisheries" (Embrace flats fishes as flagship and umbrella species, p. 290 (PDF p. 4))
- Umbrella candidates: "bonefish and Atlantic Tarpon have been previously suggested as umbrella species candidates (Boucek et al., 2019; Perez et al., 2020)" (Embrace flats fishes as flagship and umbrella species, p. 290 (PDF p. 4))
- Precautionary habitat protection: "While the causal link between the bonefish recovery following its collapse (Brownscombe et al., 2019b) remains unknown and could also be tied to recruitment or fishing effort, prioritizing habitat improvements and protection offers a precautionary approach to safeguard these fragile nearshore ecosystems (Boucek et al., 2024)." (Introduction, p. 289 (PDF p. 3))
- Sewer transition: "this catch increase coincided with an archipelago-wide initiative to transition from septic systems to central sewer systems, potentially improving water quality and, thus, supporting the recruitment and survival of juvenile bonefish (Boucek et al., 2023)" (Introduction, pp. 288 to 289 (PDF pp. 2 to 3))
- Stock assessment gap: "Given the absence of initiatives for formal stock assessments on recreational flats species, it is imperative to restore and maintain the diverse mosaic of flats habitats, which should help to support habitat function, connectivity, and integrity (Adams & Cooke, 2015)." (Introduction, p. 289 (PDF p. 3))
- Ridge to reef: "Adopting the land-to-sea continuum or ridge-to-reef approach, restoration efforts can expand beyond focal habitats and extend to watershed hydrology that support nearshore environments (J. K. Carlson et al., 2019)." (Consider connectivity, p. 290 (PDF p. 4))
- Proactive protection: "Considering the high costs of marine habitat restoration to return their functional roles (Bayraktarov et al., 2016), proactive protection of these ecosystems is essential to mitigate impacts from human activity and population growth (Y. Zeng et al., 2022)." (Insert habitat protection and restoration into development and planning processes, p. 290 (PDF p. 4))
- Everglades flows: "restoring natural freshwater flows regionally from the Florida Everglades could re-establish historic salinity levels and reduce pollutant effluents (Guardo & Tomasello, 1995; Sklar et al., 2005), thereby improving habitat quality for species like bonefish in South Florida (Brownscombe et al., 2019b)" (Insert habitat protection and restoration into development and planning processes, p. 290 (PDF p. 4))
- Angler organisations: "engaging flats anglers through habitat-dependent outdoor recreation and conservation organizations could similarly drive positive policy changes for flats habitat management and restoration" (Learn from habitat management and restoration in other realms, p. 291 (PDF p. 5))
- Permit policy example: "Brownscombe et al. (2019a) highlighted how resource users' involvement and local ecological knowledge led to a rapid policy change surrounding harvest closures for permit at spawning sites" (Engage in effective partnerships, p. 291 (PDF p. 5))
- Deep-water refuge in design: "Thus, incorporating access to deeper water into flats restoration design may increase survival rates for economically important catch-and-release fisheries like bonefish by reducing post-release predation." (Think and act at the systems level, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Seascape complexity: "By considering the habitat needs of flats species at a systems level, including natural predators and angling, more effective restoration efforts should aim for supporting seascape complexity." (Think and act at the systems level, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Mangroves and seagrass as refuge: "restoring mangrove and seagrass ecosystems offer another important strategy for reducing natural and fishery-induced predation risk" (Think and act at the systems level, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Stressors first: "preventing and mitigating existing stressors should be prioritized before and during restoration, as this is often the most effective way to facilitate natural recovery (Elliott et al., 2007)" (Emulate nature, p. 293 (PDF p. 7))
- Ecological traps: "Structural interventions, like SmartGates, though beneficial for tidal regimes, can inadvertently create barriers that disrupt the movement of fish and marine megafauna." (Emulate nature, p. 293 (PDF p. 7))
- Artificial reefs: "artificial reefs, which are often constructed to increase fish production but can function as fish attractants, making species more susceptible to harvest and failing to promote long-term population growth (Bohnsack, 1998)" (Emulate nature, p. 293 (PDF p. 7))
- Static strategies: "static restoration strategies will likely fall short of long-term restoration objectives (Harris et al., 2006)" (Future-proof restoration, p. 293 (PDF p. 7))
- Marine protected areas: "When carefully designed and enforced, MPAs have shown great potential for substantial conservation outcomes when protections extend to habitats and not just fish (Gaines et al., 2010)." (Synthesis and Conclusion, p. 294 (PDF p. 8))
- Policy simplification: "There is a need for policies that simplify and expedite processes and effectively incorporate conservation and restoration efforts into a unified legislative strategy." (Synthesis and Conclusion, p. 294 (PDF p. 8))
- Ecosystem-based management: "The synthesis of our current knowledge and needs suggests that embracing an ecosystem-based management approach is essential." (Synthesis and Conclusion, p. 294 (PDF p. 8))
- Closing position: "Despite navigating a landscape of imperfect knowledge, decisive action is needed through policy and practice to conserve flats ecosystems, their biodiversity, the fisheries they support and the ecosystem services they provide." (Synthesis and Conclusion, p. 293 (PDF p. 7))

## Stated limitations

- Data-poor species: "With only limited information available on their demographics and population statuses, effective management of flats-dependent species is difficult due to often being categorized as data poor." (Introduction, p. 288 (PDF p. 2))
- Unproven causal link: "Although empirical evidence is needed, this catch increase coincided with an archipelago-wide initiative to transition from septic systems to central sewer systems" (Introduction, pp. 288 to 289 (PDF pp. 2 to 3))
- Thin restoration evidence: "Unfortunately, for flats that are home to flats fishes, such empirical evidence on restoration effectiveness is scant." (Build the evidence base, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Reporting bias: "However, reporting bias toward successful outcomes is pervasive, driven by scientific journal interests, human factors, or the need to meet funding goals (Catalano et al., 2019; Eger et al., 2022)." (Build the evidence base, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Work outside research: "much of the current restoration work occurs outside formal research, highlighting the importance of capturing the experiences of practitioners" (Build the evidence base, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Restoration failure rate: "Restoration activities fail too often (Suding, 2011) such that there is much need to improve effectiveness." (Embrace transdisciplinary perspectives, p. 293 (PDF p. 7))
- Predator control not feasible: "This approach is not feasible when conflicts arise with protected predators; for example, marine mammal and some shark species in marine systems (R. R. Carlson et al., 2019)." (Think and act at the systems level, p. 292 (PDF p. 6))
- Uncertainty acknowledged: "Collectively, these 10 guiding principles, while grounded in current understanding, also acknowledge the uncertainties and complexities inherent in ecosystem management." (Synthesis and Conclusion, p. 294 (PDF p. 8))
- Missing citation in text: "Adapting to contemporary disturbances regimes like altered precipitation and intensifying storms can enhance the resilience of habitats and their species ( )." (Future-proof restoration, p. 293 (PDF p. 7)); the parenthetical citation is empty in the published text.
