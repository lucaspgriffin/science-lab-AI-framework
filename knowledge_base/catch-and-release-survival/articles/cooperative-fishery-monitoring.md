# Cooperative fishery monitoring

## Summary

Griffin et al. (2021) describe how a fly-fishing operator and a local conservation organisation in the Alphonse Group, Republic of Seychelles, turned a guide-kept catch count into a spatially explicit monitoring programme for a catch-and-release fishery. The partners were the Alphonse Fishing Company and the Island Conservation Society [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 1]. The company had recorded catch numbers since 2012, and the two organisations began collecting more detailed information in 2017 [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. From that point guides logged species, fork length, time of day, and capture location for five target species, using GPS watches and a named map of the fishing grounds [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4].

Over three fishing seasons the programme recorded 684 giant trevally (*Caranx ignobilis*), 141 moustache triggerfish (*Balistoides viridescens*), 99 Indo-Pacific permit (*Trachinotus blochii*), 55 milkfish (*Chanos chanos*), and 46 yellowmargin triggerfish (*Pseudobalistes flavimarginatus*) [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 1]. Reported catch rose across seasons for giant trevally, permit, and moustache triggerfish and fell for milkfish and yellowmargin triggerfish [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. Angling effort was not recorded, so the analyses rest on raw counts, body size, and catch-location kernels rather than catch per unit effort [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. The paper presents the programme as a foundation for further research, for a code of conduct already in use, and for formal co-management of the fishery [Griffin et al. 2021, pp. 1, 8].

## Key points

### Study system

The Republic of Seychelles comprises 115 islands in the Western Indian Ocean, and its exclusive economic zone spans nearly 1.4 million km² [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 3]. Travel and tourism accounted for 40.5% of gross domestic production in 2019 [World Travel and Tourism Council 2020, cited in Griffin et al. 2021, p. 3]. The Alphonse Group lies in the Outer Islands, 87 km south of the Amirantes Ridge, and consists of two low-lying coralline atolls separated by a 2.4 km channel, with oceanic water deeper than 2000 m only 7–10 km from the coral plateaus [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 3].

Alphonse Atoll has a 174 ha island, a 540 ha lagoon, and 402 ha of peripheral reef flats [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 3]. St. François Atoll holds the islands of Bijoutier (nearly 2 ha) and St. François (17 ha), a 1650 ha lagoon, and 3732 ha of reef flats [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 3]. Both atolls have a shallow central lagoon under 10 m deep surrounded by flats of sand, coral rubble, and seagrass [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 3].

### Fishing effort structure

Fly fishing runs during the northwest monsoon, "primarily September–May" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 3]. Since 2012 bookings have been capped at a maximum of 12 anglers per week; the cap now stands at 10 pre-sold anglers plus 2 rods for on-site sale that are used 50% of the time [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. A weekly package provides six guided days on St. François, with two guests paired per guide, fishing from a 4.9 m flats skiff or by wading [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. The effort is structured and capped, but the logs did not capture how it was spent.

### Species covered

The operation targets seven species: giant trevally, bluefin trevally (*Caranx melampygus*), bonefish (*Albula glossodonta*), Indo-Pacific permit, moustache triggerfish, yellowmargin triggerfish, and milkfish [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Five were monitored. Bonefish were left out despite being a main target, because "it was deemed too cumbersome to include monitoring of A. glossodonta", with catches that can exceed 20 per angler per day [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. The text does not state why bluefin trevally were not monitored.

### Records per fish

From September 2017 the partners recorded the total number of each monitored species caught by guests per day [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Guides wore Garmin Quatix III GPS watches to mark capture locations, managed in Garmin BaseCamp software, and "also recorded information about the fish (species, fork length), time of day, and, occasionally, other environmental conditions (cloud cover, wind direction, wind strength, wave action, tidal phase)" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. When a watch was not used, location came from a map of 66 named flats and channels, which "could be used to provide coarse spatial data for capture location" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Substituted locations "were re-reviewed by the fishing guides to ensure accuracy" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4].

### Missing effort data

The logs counted fish but not angler effort: "no angling effort was readily recorded, thus, not allowing for catch per unit calculations" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. No catch per unit effort series exists for this period. Trends in the paper are trends in reported catch, and the authors caution against reading them as abundance (see Catch interpretation below).

### Dock exclusion

All giant trevally caught at the main dock of Alphonse Atoll "were removed from all reporting and analyses" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Three reasons are given: these fish "were 1) often captured in atypical fashion (i.e., bait), 2) used to evaluate C&R angling effects related to a separate study, and 3) are considered to be habitual to the location because it sometimes serves as a feeding station by guests" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. The rule keeps a provisioned, repeatedly sampled aggregation from inflating counts and catch-area kernels for the open-flats fishery.

### Catch counts

Season-to-season change was not monotonic for three species: permit fell 45.5% and then rose 166.7%, moustache triggerfish fell 53.8% and then rose 170.8%, and yellowmargin triggerfish fell 60% and then rose 125% [Griffin et al. 2021, Table 1, p. 5]. Giant trevally rose 153.8% and then 19.7% [Griffin et al. 2021, Table 1, p. 5].

### Body size

Fork length ranged from 20–140 cm for giant trevally (n = 657, 75.5 ± 14.7 cm), 43–130 cm for milkfish (n = 29, 96.1 ± 15.3 cm), 20–76 cm for permit (n = 81, 50.1 ± 15.4 cm), 15–58 cm for moustache triggerfish (n = 109, 32.8 ± 9.2 cm), and 20–72 cm for yellowmargin triggerfish (n = 38, 39.9 ± 12.2 cm) [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. Size did not differ among seasons for giant trevally (H(2) = 0.45, p = 0.8) or milkfish (H(2) = 2.72, p = 0.26) [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5].

Permit declined in size (H(2) = 7.39, p = 0.03, effect size 0.69) between 2017–2018 (n = 27, 57.4 ± 9.66) and 2019–2020 (n = 37, 44.8 ± 17.6); units are not printed for these means [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. Moustache triggerfish are reported twice in the same paragraph: once with no seasonal difference (F(2,35) = 2.12, p = 0.14) and once with a decrease (H(2) = 6.98, p = 0.03, effect size 0.05) from 34.6 ± 8.9 cm to 30 ± 9.43 cm [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. The paper does not say which test applies, so the triggerfish size trend should be treated as unresolved.

### Monthly catch

All count models, with fishing season as a random effect, showed monthly variation in catch [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. Predicted peaks were February (ŷ = 46, CI 22 to 96) and January (ŷ = 33, CI 16 to 69) for giant trevally; December (ŷ = 6, CI 3 to 11) and January (ŷ = 5, CI 3 to 8) for milkfish; October (ŷ = 8, CI 5 to 13) for permit; October (ŷ = 10, CI 6 to 17) and November (ŷ = 8, CI 5 to 14) for moustache triggerfish; and February (ŷ = 3, CI 2 to 6) for yellowmargin triggerfish [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. The authors note that "variance was relatively high, due to sample size" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5].

### Catch areas

Mean kernel density catch areas were largest for giant trevally (25.3 ± 17 km²), then moustache triggerfish (10.8 ± 6.55 km²), permit (6.44 ± 4.93 km²), yellowmargin triggerfish (4.51 ± 2.81 km²), and milkfish (4.18 ± 4.24 km²) [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. Giant trevally catches concentrated on the outer atoll edge and on shallow flats bordered by deeper lagoon water; milkfish on the outer edge; permit on interior shallow flats; and both triggerfish followed the giant trevally pattern [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. Areas of highest catch density were "fairly consistent across the three fishing seasons for all species", with some seasonal shifts in core areas [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5].

### Catch-area overlap

Overlap was defined as "the proportion of species i's KDE that is overlapped by species j's KDE" [Kernohan et al. 2001, cited in Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. The metric is asymmetric: a species with a small catch area nested inside a larger one shows high overlap, while the reverse does not. Mean overlap was 0.79 (±0.19) for 95% kernels and 0.60 (±0.31) for 50% core kernels [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. In the 95% matrix, 0.96 of the yellowmargin triggerfish kernel fell within the giant trevally kernel, whereas only 0.41 of the giant trevally kernel fell within the yellowmargin triggerfish kernel [Griffin et al. 2021, Table 2a, p. 9]. At the core level, only 0.09 of the milkfish core area overlapped the yellowmargin triggerfish core area [Griffin et al. 2021, Table 2b, p. 9].

The paper reports per-species overlap "of core catch areas" as greatest for giant trevally (0.94 ± 0.05), then milkfish (0.87 ± 0.14), moustache triggerfish (0.82 ± 0.17), permit (0.68 ± 0.21), and yellowmargin triggerfish (0.63 ± 0.22) [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. These values match the column means of the 95% matrix in Table 2a, diagonal included, and not the 50% matrix in Table 2b. They describe how much of the other species' catch areas each species' kernel covers, at the 95% level. Any reuse should cite the matrix directly.

### Catch interpretation

The authors warn against reading rising giant trevally catches as a healthy stock: "fish populations have been documented to exhibit hyperstability, in which catch per unit effort remains stable or increases as true abundances decline" [Hilborn and Walters 1992, cited in Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. Guide and angler skill compound the problem, since "As guide and angler experience and/or effort increases, catch rates may as well, masking the actual trends in the population, thus, adopting monitoring of both catch data and effort is warranted" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. Rising catch may also indicate "an increased sensitivity of some species to fishing pressure" [Post et al. 2002, cited in Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5].

Stable size across seasons is read cautiously as a sign that some of these fisheries "may be more robust to fishing pressure since anglers are capturing a similar sized and aged fish from season-to-season", with more data needed for confirmation [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5]. The permit and triggerfish size declines "may be more related to their ecology (e.g., small home ranges, territorial)" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5].

### Management uses

The programme fed a code of conduct for catch-and-release. Its content includes "barbless hooks, stronger tackle to reduce fight times, rubberized mesh nets, how-to instructions for photographs, release and reviving fish strategies, and general environmental awareness practices for other species (i.e., corals, turtles, birds, predators) and their habitats" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Catch maps were judged "useful to help guide potential management solutions that include temporarily closing areas from fishing activities" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 6]. In early 2020, based on movement tracking data and preliminary catch data, the partners "agreed to close down two separate high-pressure fishing locations to experimentally examine and quantify the role of closures" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 7].

The wider context includes the designation of 30% of the Seychelles exclusive economic zone as marine protected areas in 2020, covering several Outer Islands [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 8]. The authors expect such cooperative initiatives "may lead to formal co-management structures that benefit multiple stakeholder groups" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 8].

### Expansion risk

As giant trevally operations spread to less-pressured Outer Islands such as Cosmoledo, Farquhar, Astove, and Providence, "initial exceptionally high catch rates may lead to severe declines in catchability, followed by a continued slow decline, as observed in other fisheries" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 6]. A baseline log of the kind built at Alphonse is the record against which such a decline could be detected.

### Survival gap

For all five monitored species, "there have been no studies that confirm their post-release survival and, ultimately, their suitability as C&R fisheries" [Cooke and Suski 2005, cited in Griffin et al. 2021, p. 6]. Survival evaluation for giant trevally "is needed and has been identified as a major knowledge gap surrounding this species" [Grabowski and Franklin 2017, cited in Griffin et al. 2021, p. 6]. For permit, the closely related Atlantic permit (*Trachinotus falcatus*) in the Florida Keys was robust to angling effects where predator presence was low [Holder et al. 2020a, cited in Griffin et al. 2021, p. 6]. The suitability of milkfish and both triggerfish for catch-and-release "is still unknown" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 6].

### Operating constraints

Guide buy-in was essential. Guides were briefed "through presentations and one-on-one conversations, so that, in turn, they could effectively advocate and communicate with their clients on why extra time was spent to correctly fulfill monitoring procedures" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 8]. Some clients "were found to become agitated or impatient" when measurement cut into fishing time or raised fish welfare concerns [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 8]. Tipping creates a conflict of interest, because data collection competes with client experience "when guides are heavily compensated through tipping practices" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 8]. Technical and logistical problems sometimes forced guides to give a general location rather than coordinates [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4].

## Methods and approaches

The analysis pipeline is reusable for any guide-kept catch log with locations and lengths. All analyses used R 3.6.2, with code at the lead author's GitHub account [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Summaries are reported as mean ± standard deviation [Griffin et al. 2021, pp. 4–5].

- **Data filters.** Remove catches from provisioned or experimentally sampled sites, as with the Alphonse dock rule, before computing counts or kernels [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Flag records whose location came from the named-flats map rather than GPS, and have guides re-check them [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4].
- **Size by season.** One-way repeated measures analysis of variance, or the Kruskal-Wallis test "when statistical assumptions (e.g., large outliers, homogeneity of variance, large deviations from normality) were violated", run with rstatix [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Report which test was used for each species, given the conflicting triggerfish results in the paper.
- **Monthly counts.** Generalized linear mixed models in glmmTMB relating month to catch count per species, with fishing season as the random effect [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Poisson, negative binomial, or quasi-Poisson distributions were chosen by Akaike information criterion with MuMIn, and by overdispersion checks with performance [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Interpretation, validation, and predictions used sjPlot, sjstats, and performance [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Predicted counts were plotted with 95% confidence intervals [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 7].
- **Catch-area kernels.** Build 99%, 95%, and 50% kernel density estimates in adehabitatHR, with "the 50% catch data KDE" defined as "the core catch area for the given species" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. A 150 m smoothing parameter was chosen "to account for the uncertainty around some catch locations, i.e., when locational data was not recorded via GPS but derived from generalized fishing locations" [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Areas came from the kernel.area function [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4]. Mapping used ggplot2 and tmap [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4].
- **Overlap.** Directional proportion of species i's kernel covered by species j's kernel, reported as a full matrix at the 95% and 50% levels [Kernohan et al. 2001, cited in Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4; Griffin et al. 2021, Table 2, p. 9].
- **Percent change.** Overall change between the first and last season, with season-to-season change reported alongside [Griffin et al. 2021, Table 1, p. 5].
- **Effort fields to add.** The authors recommend "modified catch logs to record number of daily fishing guests, their own and their guide expertise level, and their proportion of daily effort across target species" so that catch per unit effort can be calculated directly [Griffin et al. 2021, pp. 7–8]. Spatial effort is worth recording even when nothing is caught, because pressure may make fish "'educated', hook shy" or responsive to boat noise [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 8].
- **High-volume species.** Where per-fish logging is impractical, as with bonefish, seine nets or traditional artisanal fish traps "could be paired with coarse catch log estimates" [Boucek et al. 2019; Filous et al. 2021, cited in Griffin et al. 2021, p. 7].

## Open questions

- Without effort, the 203.8% rise in giant trevally catch cannot be separated from growth in angler numbers, guide skill, or targeting, and hyperstability remains possible [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5].
- Whether the permit and moustache triggerfish size declines reflect fishing-induced size selection or local ecology needs continued monitoring; the triggerfish result is also internally inconsistent [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5].
- Post-release survival is unmeasured for all five monitored species, so their suitability for catch-and-release is assumed rather than shown [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 6].
- The results of the two experimental closures from early 2020 are not reported in this paper [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 7].
- The methods give an end date of May 2019 while the results name a 2019–2020 season, and the text does not reconcile the two [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 4].
- Bonefish, a main target species, remain unmonitored in this programme [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 7].
- Per-season kernel areas (Table S2) and model coefficients (Table S1) sit in the supplement and were not available [Griffin et al. 2021, p. 5].
