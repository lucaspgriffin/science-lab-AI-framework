# Angling pressure and catchability

## Summary

Catch-and-release (C&R) fisheries are usually evaluated by whether released fish survive. Repeated angler-fish interactions can also change how fish use space and how easily they are caught again, which in turn sets how much fishing pressure a fishery can sustain. Griffin et al. (2024) tested this for giant trevally (*Caranx ignobilis*) in the Alphonse Island Group, Seychelles, by combining acoustic telemetry, PIT-tag mark-and-recapture, and guide catch logs [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 1]. The study tested habituation, an effect of the temporary fishing closure during COVID-19, and timidity [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 2].

Adults resident at a floating dock, classed as habituated, had 50% and 95% home ranges about half the size of those of non-habituated adults and were more likely to make restricted movements during the fishing season [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 6, 8]. The closure produced no detectable change in movement [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8]. Non-habituated fish became less likely to make restricted movements with each successive fishing season [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 10]. Recapture rates were 65% for potential habituated fish and 4% for non-habituated fish, even though the core catch area overlapped 30% of the 95% telemetry home range on Saint François [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 10, 11]. The authors interpreted the low recapture rate as hook shyness and concluded that fishery capacity "may be at saturation" [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 14].

## Key points

### Study system

The Alphonse Group consists of Alphonse and St. François atolls, separated by a 2.5 km channel, with shallow (<10 m) lagoons of 540 and 1650 ha and reef flats of 402 and 3732 ha, respectively [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 3]. The group lies 150 km from the nearest atoll [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 14]. A 62 m floating dock at Alphonse Island, used to load guests and equipment, is the high human use area around which habituation was assessed [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 3].

Sixty-eight VR2W receivers were deployed at 1.5–11 m, and the seven receivers around the dock were pooled into one station group [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 3]. Between May and November 2019, the 50% detection range averaged 293 m and the maximum range (5% detection efficiency) was 363 m [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 3]. Of 74 fish tagged with acoustic transmitters between October 2018 and December 2019, 66 were detected and 53 were retained for analysis [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 3, 5, 6].

### Habituation classification

Habituation was defined by dock residency. In the paper's words, "Any dock residency greater than 0.5 was considered “habituated”" [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 4]. Eleven of the 53 fish had dock residency of at least 0.01, and eight exceeded 0.5 (0.89 ± 0.10, range 0.65–0.97) [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 6]. The eight habituated fish were all mature (109 ± 20 cm FL) [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 6]. Non-habituated fish comprised 40 adults (79.05 ± 11.47 cm FL) and five juveniles (57 ± 3 cm FL), split at 60 cm FL, the length at which 50% of fish are sexually mature according to Sudekum (1991) [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 4, 6].

The habituated group was small and consisted of large fish. The authors stated that dock residency "cannot be definitively attributed to food provisioning," because provisioning had been limited in recent years and fish may also be drawn to structure or respond to seasonal and environmental factors [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 12].

### Home range

One non-habituated adult (Fish ID 27) was dropped for too few unique locations, leaving 39 non-habituated adults for home range estimation [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 6]. The Fig. 2 caption reports 38 non-habituated adults, which does not match the text; the inconsistency is in the source [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 7]. The ANOVA degrees of freedom, F(2, 49), are consistent with 52 fish, that is, 8 habituated, 39 non-habituated adults, and 5 juveniles [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 6].

Home range differed among the three groups for both the 50% (F(2, 49) = 5.22, p = 0.01) and 95% (F(2, 49) = 3.63, p = 0.03) estimates [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 6]. Mean areas (km2, ± SD) were [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 7]:

| Group | 50% home range | 95% home range |
|---|---|---|
| Habituated | 0.27 ± 0.06 | 2.65 ± 0.43 |
| Non-habituated adult | 0.62 ± 0.34 | 6.19 ± 3.88 |
| Non-habituated juvenile | 0.46 ± 0.26 | 5.12 ± 2.75 |

Non-habituated adults had 50% home ranges 1.97 times (95% CI 1.17–3.32) and 95% home ranges 1.95 times (95% CI 1.08–3.53) those of habituated fish [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 6].

### Regional connectivity

Across the 53 retained fish, 36% (n = 19) were detected in all three regions (Alphonse Atoll, Bijoutier Island, and Saint François) [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 6, 8]. Six habituated fish (75%) were detected only at Alphonse Atoll and two (25%) at Alphonse Atoll and Bijoutier Island; all of their 95% location estimates fell within Alphonse Atoll [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 6, 8]. Juvenile 95% estimates fell only within Saint François Atoll, where they were tagged [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8].

For adults, the source reports that 95% estimates "predominantly fell within the atoll of initial tagging," with 15% (n = 6) spanning both atolls and one fish (3%) with a 50% core area across both [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8]. The source labels this group "habituated adults (n = 39)." That label is an error in the source: the sample size and context show the sentence refers to the non-habituated adults [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8].

### Habitat residency and activity

All eight habituated fish had lagoon residency above 0.9 [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8]. Among the 40 non-habituated adults, 23% (n = 9) had lagoon residency above 0.75 and 70% (n = 28) had reef residency above 0.75 [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8]. One juvenile had lagoon residency of 0.98; the other four had reef residency of 0.89, 1, 0.81, and 0.6 [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8].

Accelerometer data did not separate the groups. Mean acceleration was 0.70 ± 0.07 m s−2 for six habituated fish and 0.78 ± 0.11 m s−2 for six non-habituated adults (t(9.95) = −1.20, p = 0.26) [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8]. Habituated fish did not differ between the fishing season (0.71 ± 0.07 m s−2) and the off-season (0.57 ± 0.10 m s−2) (t(6.31) = −1.80, p = 0.12) [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8].

### Movement models

Each binomial mixed model classified gaps between detections as restricted or out-of-range movements, and each tested one hypothesis.

- **Habituation.** Habituated fish during the fishing season were more likely to make restricted movements (interaction β = 0.33, p = 0.001) [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8]. Marginal R2 was 0.04 and conditional R2 0.18, with individual and station explaining 34% and 66% of random-effect variance; the holdout AUC was 0.75 [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8]. Half (n = 3) of habituated fish and 56% (n = 20) of non-habituated adults departed from the intercept, whereas no juvenile did [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8].
- **Closure.** Comparing April–May of 2019, 2020 (the COVID-19 closure), and 2021, only nearest station distance was significant (p = 0.03), with a negligible coefficient (β < 0.001) [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 5, 8]. Marginal R2 was 0.003, conditional R2 0.17, and AUC 0.68; individual and station explained 43% and 57% of random-effect variance [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 8, 10].
- **Timidity.** Relative to the 2019–2020 season, the 2020–2021 (β = −0.21) and 2021–2022 (β = −0.31) seasons were significant (p < 0.001), indicating a declining probability of restricted movements with each season [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 10]. Marginal R2 was 0.005, conditional R2 0.13, and AUC 0.65; individual and station explained 39% and 61% of random-effect variance [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 10].

Fixed effects explained little variance in all three models, and individual and station differences accounted for most of the explained variation. The habituation interaction is the clearest effect. The closure and timidity results rest on small marginal R2 values.

### Recapture and catch overlap

From January 2017 through July 2023, 1952 giant trevally were caught and 1044 were PIT-tagged, yielding 62 recaptures (5.9%) [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 10]. Recapture rates differed by group [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 11]:

| Group | Caught | Tagged | Recapture events | Rate | Recaptured once, twice, three times |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Non-habituated | 1890 | 1007 | 38 | 0.04 | 38, 0, 0 |
| Potential habituated | 62 | 37 | 24 | 0.65 | 16, 6, 2 |
| Overall | 1952 | 1044 | 62 | 0.06 | 54, 6, 2 |

No non-habituated fish was recaptured more than once, and non-habituated recaptures averaged 4.2 km (±3.9 km) and 16 days (±99 days) from first capture [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 10]. Recaptured and non-recaptured non-habituated fish did not differ in length (71 ± 9 cm, n = 37, against 73 ± 14 cm, n = 954; t(43.64) = −2.00, p = 0.52) [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 10–11].

Only 831 PIT-tagged fish had matching guide catch-log locations [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 11]. The core catch area (50% KDE) was 15.07 km2 and the broader catch area (95% KDE) 61.51 km2 [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 11]. Against the 21.27 km2 95% telemetry home range on Saint François, overlap was 30% (6.29 km2) for the core catch area and 84% (17.94 km2) for the broader catch area [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 11]. Fish therefore spent much of their time where anglers fished but were rarely caught twice, which the authors read as hook shyness [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 1].

### Management implications

The authors concluded that "the fishery capacity within the Alphonse Group may be at saturation, characterized by a sustained level of hook shyness in GT, which is unlikely to change without the introduction of new individuals to the population or reduction in fishing pressure" [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 14]. They warned that "adding angler pressure may lead to greater spatial avoidance and hook shyness" [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 15].

Fishing closures are the main lever this study speaks to. A closure of April–May 2020 did not shift movement patterns, and the authors called for "additional research to determine the minimum duration of fishing closure that would be needed to elicit observable shifts in GT behavior" [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 14]. A short closure should not be expected to reset catchability.

Other recommendations were to retain limits on anglers per day and strict handling guidelines, credited with balancing angler pressure and satisfaction; to spread effort across multiple species to extend capacity for giant trevally; to evaluate catch per unit effort for each species alongside these methods; and to study spawning and larval recruitment in the region [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 14–15].

## Methods and approaches

The workflow is reusable for any C&R fishery with an acoustic array and a tag-and-release program.

1. **Detection filtering.** Remove consecutive detections less than 60 s apart (the minimum tag delay), movements above 10 m s−1 from straight-line distances, and singular detections within 120 min [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 4]. Plot and inspect fish with 25 or fewer monitoring days or days detected, or with detections on five or fewer stations, to screen for mortality or emigration [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 4].
2. **Residency and classification.** Build detection events with `glatos::detection_events` using a 60 min threshold, compute the Kessel residence index with `glatos::residence_index`, and classify fish with dock residency above 0.5 as habituated [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 4]. Split adults and juveniles at 60 cm FL [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 4].
3. **Home range.** Compute centers of activity with the mean position algorithm in 60 min bins (`VTrack`), then kernel utilization distributions with 200 m smoothing bands (`adehabitatHR`), and extract 50% and 95% contours [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 4]. Compare groups by ANOVA. See [[home-range-and-utilisation-distributions]] for estimator choice.
4. **Activity.** Compare mean acceleration with Welch two-sample t tests, defining the fishing season as September–May and the off-season as June–August [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 4]. Acceleration was sampled at 5 Hz for 5 s per transmission cycle [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 3].
5. **Gap analysis.** Drop recursion gaps under 6 min [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 4]. Compute RDET as the observed gap relative to the expected time between detections, the distance between recursions divided by mean swim speed from the 12 accelerometer-tagged fish, and drop movements with RDET > 5 [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 4–5]. Split gaps into restricted and out-of-range movements with Fisher natural breaks (`classInt::classIntervals`) [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 4]. Thresholds were 44 min for recursions and 0.25 RDET for transitions in the habituation analysis, and 60 min and 0.19 RDET at Saint François in the closure analysis [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8].
6. **Mixed models.** Fit binomial (logit) models in `glmmTMB` with movement type as the response and random intercepts for individual and station [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 5]. The habituation model crossed fish classification with fishing season (during or outside) [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 5]. The closure model compared April–May of 2019, 2020 (reference), and 2021, with length class (small adults 60–100 cm FL, large adults >100 cm FL, updated over time from the Sudekum growth curve) and nearest station distance from station-year distance matrices [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 5]. The timidity model used fishing seasons September 2019–May 2020 (reference), September 2020–May 2021, and September 2021–May 2022 [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 5]. Six fish without detections in both the on- and off-season were removed [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 8].
7. **Validation.** Train on 60% of the data and compute AUC on the 40% holdout with `pROC`; check residuals with 10 000 simulations in `DHARMa` [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 5].
8. **Catch overlap.** Smooth catch-log locations (GPS watches, 15 m accuracy) with a 200 m parameter to absorb location uncertainty, build 50% and 95% catch KDEs, and compute the percentage of telemetry space use covered by each [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 3, 5].

All analyses ran in R 4.2.2, with summary statistics as mean ± standard deviation and α = 0.05 [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 3].

## Open questions

- **Closure duration.** The minimum closure that would produce a measurable behavioural shift is unknown [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 14].
- **Habituation cause.** Dock residency may reflect provisioning, structure, or environmental factors, and the design cannot separate them [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 12].
- **Alternative explanations for low recapture.** Population size, satiation, and body condition were not measured, and a larger population would itself lower recapture probability [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 14]. The authors also noted that "claims of hook shyness might be overstated to rationalize unsuccessful fishing" [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 14].
- **Sampling bias.** Uneven angling effort and depth limits on receiver placement may bias home range estimates and angler-fish overlap [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 14]. Detection range was likely lower along the reef, and receiver losses required annual array changes [Griffin et al. 2024, p. 3].
- **Open population.** Immigration and emigration are possible, and recruitment to the local population is poorly known [Griffin et al. 2024, pp. 14–15].
