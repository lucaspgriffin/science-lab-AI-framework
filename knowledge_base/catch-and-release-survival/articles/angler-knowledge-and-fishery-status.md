# Tarpon angler knowledge

## Summary

Atlantic tarpon (*Megalops atlanticus*) support an economically important recreational fishery with little formal information on population or fishery status. Griffin et al. (2023, *Marine Policy*) used an online survey of anglers and fishing guides to collect local ecological knowledge on current and historical fishing quality, migration timing, perceived threats, shark depredation, and support for conservation measures. Of 1128 responses, 918 completed surveys were analysed [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3]. Respondents who had fished since the 1970s reported a significant decline in fishing quality from good to fair, water and habitat quality ranked as the greatest perceived threats, and guides lost an estimated 2–7 tarpon per guide per season to sharks depending on gear type [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4, p. 12]. Guides and anglers largely agreed on threats, whereas fly and spin respondents differed on several regulations. The sample was non-random, mostly fly anglers resident in the United States, and English-only, which the authors treat as the main constraint on inference [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 12–13].

## Key points

### Survey design

The instrument was an online Qualtrics survey of predominantly closed-ended questions that took approximately 12 minutes to complete, approved by the University of Massachusetts Amherst Institutional Review Board (Protocol ID 2019–2196) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 2]. Eligibility required respondents to be 18 years or older with Atlantic tarpon angling experience. The survey ran from 9 September 2020 to 8 August 2021 and was available only in English, which the authors flag as relevant because Spanish is the primary language in parts of the fishery, such as Mexico and Cuba [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 2].

The survey had three sections: angler behaviour, fishing quality and conservation concerns, and demographics [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 2]. Behaviour items covered region and habitat fished, month, years of experience, annual days on the water, guide status, gear type (spin or fly), and the number of tarpon hooked and landed. Respondents also rated six personal identities (environmentalist, outdoorsperson, catch-and-release angler, angler, hunter, conservationist) on a five-point Likert scale [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 2].

### Distribution and sampling

Distribution "relied on a snowball sampling approach using social media and relevant e-newsletters" [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 2]. The survey was shared on Twitter, Instagram, and Facebook by the authors and by angling organizations, including Bonefish & Tarpon Trust and fishing clubs, and appeared regularly in the monthly Bonefish & Tarpon Trust e-newsletter. A raffle for a backpack served as an optional incentive [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 2]. No response rate can be computed because the size of the sampled population is unknown [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 13].

### Respondents by group

Of the completed surveys, 90% (n = 824) came from anglers not employed as guides, and 71.87% (n = 654) fished primarily with fly gear [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3]. Among the 914 respondents who answered the guide question, 64 (7.00%) were full-time guides, 16 (1.75%) part-time guides, and 10 (1.09%) seasonal guides [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. Spin fishing with bait accounted for 184 respondents (20.22%) and spin fishing with artificial lures for 72 (7.91%) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4].

Experience was skewed toward long-term participants: 343 of 890 respondents (38.54%) reported 20 or more years of tarpon angling, and 137 (15.39%) fewer than 5 years [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. Effort was modest for most respondents, with 435 of 811 (53.64%) fishing fewer than 15 days per year for tarpon and 310 (38.22%) fishing 15–49 days [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. Respondents were predominantly men over the age of 45; 842 of 875 (96.23%) identified as male, and 309 of 906 (34.11%) were 65 or older [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4].

### Respondents by region

Most respondents fished the Gulf of Mexico within the United States, including the Florida Keys (n = 589, 67.39%), followed by the Southeastern United States Atlantic coast (n = 159, 18.19%), Central America including Mexico (n = 82, 9.38%), and the Caribbean (n = 44, 5.03%); none reported Africa or South America as their primary location [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. Residence was even more concentrated, with 881 of 915 respondents (96.28%) living in the United States [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. Within the United States, effort centred on the Florida Keys (n = 240), West Florida between Marco Island and Apalachee Bay (n = 212), and the Everglades (n = 94) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. Fishing pressure peaked in May and June, driven by the Gulf of Mexico, and in June through August in the Southeastern United States [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4].

### Perceived trends

Overall change in fishing quality since respondents began fishing was best explained by guide status, years of experience, days fished, location, and gear type (Nagelkerke pseudo R² = 0.29) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. Most respondents felt quality had remained the same, "with the exception of guides with more than 20 years of Atlantic tarpon angling experience, who were more likely to report that fishing quality had declined" [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. Anglers rated quality better than guides did (p = 0.025), spin respondents better than fly respondents (p = 0.017), and respondents in the Caribbean (p = 0.03) and Southeastern United States (p < 0.001) were more likely than those in the Gulf of Mexico to report improvement [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4].

Decadal ratings came from a much smaller subset: 158 of 918 respondents rated quality from the 1970s through the 2010s, and they reported a significant decline from good to fair (t = 8.81, df = 307.99, p < 0.001) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. Guides and anglers in this subset did not differ in perceived change (t = −0.49, df = 31.86, p = 0.63). Across decades, most respondents still rated fishing as good to very good, "though the proportion of respondents who felt the fishing quality was fair to very poor did increase over time" [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4].

Migration timing responses were modelled with guide status, experience, days on the water, and location (Nagelkerke pseudo R² = 0.20) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4]. More experienced anglers were more likely to report an earlier migration and less experienced anglers a later one; respondents with fewer than five years differed from those with 10–14 years (p = 0.030) and 15–19 years (p = 0.011). Respondents in the United States Gulf of Mexico (p = 0.013) and Southeastern United States (p = 0.016) differed from those in Central America [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4].

### Perceived threats

Declining water and habitat quality were "the greatest concern to both anglers and guides," and differing harvest regulations across state and country boundaries the least [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5]. Guides and anglers did not differ on seven of the eight threats (Mann-Whitney U range 32,982–40,477, p > 0.05). Shark predation related to angling events was marginally different (U = 32,247, p = 0.058), with guides rating it a very high threat more often than anglers [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5].

Gear type separated threat perceptions more than guide status did. Fly and spin respondents agreed that water quality was a very high threat and that shark predation related to angling events was a medium threat (p > 0.05) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5]. Fly respondents rated habitat decline (U = 93,968, p < 0.001) and non-angling recreational activities (U = 100,000, p < 0.001) as greater threats. Spin respondents were less concerned about angling pressure, angler ethics, differing harvest regulations, and insufficient regulations (U range 91,463–100,404, p < 0.001) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5].

### Shark depredation

Guide-reported losses were estimated separately by gear. For 58 fly guides, between 111 and 242 tarpon were lost to sharks in an average season within the last five years, or 1.9–4.1 per guide per season; for 32 spin guides, between 133 and 219 tarpon, or 4.2–6.8 per guide per season [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 6]. The ordinal model of losses included guide status, experience, days fished, location, gear, and tarpon landed per year (Nagelkerke pseudo R² = 0.31), but only guide status and tarpon landed remained significant after pairwise comparisons [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 6]. Guides were more likely to experience depredation (p = 0.0053), and respondents landing fewer than five tarpon per year were more likely to report zero shark encounters (six pairwise comparisons, p < 0.03) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 6–8].

Most respondents reported no change in losses to sharks over the last five years, and very few reported a decrease [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 8]. The trend model (Nagelkerke pseudo R² = 0.18) retained gear type and location after pairwise comparisons: Central American respondents were less likely to report change than Southeastern United States respondents (p = 0.031), and spin respondents more likely than fly respondents (p = 0.007). The likelihood of reporting an increase was highest in the Southeastern United States and the United States Gulf Coast [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 8]. Respondents ranked habitats for shark encounter risk as passes > bridges = nearshore > estuaries = rivers (p < 0.001 for significant ranks) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 8].

The authors consider these counts conservative, because "shark predation may occur immediately or hours after release" and anglers therefore "are likely underestimating how many Atlantic tarpon are lost to sharks" [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 12]. Assigning 25 as the upper value for the open-ended "20 +" bin adds a second source of underestimation [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3].

### Conservation support

Anglers and guides "almost unanimously expressed very high support" for catch-and-release only regulations, water quality management solutions, and increased science on tarpon ecology, and strongly disagreed that no conservation efforts were needed [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5]. Most gave medium to very high support to pole-troll zones, defined as areas "where high speed motorboat travel is prohibited," while opinions on closures around select bridges and passes were evenly split [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5]. Both groups rated individuals, guide associations, and state governments as more responsible for tarpon conservation than federal governments and non-governmental organizations [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5].

### Guides and anglers

Guides and anglers agreed on six conservation items (U range 32,258–35,495, p > 0.05) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5]. A larger proportion of guides expressed very high support for strict handling guidelines, expanded commercial or recreational shark harvest, targeted shark culls, and license fees for out-of-state guides (four tests, U range 26,387–31,168, p < 0.01). Anglers more often expressed very high support for ending the use of dead bait (U = 41,818, p = 0.018) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5].

### Fly and spin anglers

Fly respondents more often expressed very high support for catch-and-release only regulations, pole-troll only zones, applied ecological research, strict handling guidelines, and water quality management, although spin respondents also largely supported these measures (U range 88,496–99,958, p < 0.01) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5]. The most polarizing items were a ban on dead bait (U = 130,332, p < 0.001) and bridge or pass closures (U = 124,172, p < 0.001), which spin anglers largely opposed and fly anglers largely supported. Both groups gave very low or low support to expanded shark harvest and targeted shark culls [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 5–6]. Fly respondents had more tarpon angling experience (U = 86,873, p = 0.03) and a higher aggregate conservation identity value (U = 97,964, p < 0.001) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 6].

### Sampling limits

The authors state several limits of the non-random design. Online distribution "can introduce a bias toward digitally literate individuals" [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 2]. Over 70% of respondents fished primarily with fly gear, a bias the authors attribute to distribution mainly through Bonefish & Tarpon Trust, a fly-fishing-oriented organization, and responses "may be biased toward this organization's mission and priority conservation concerns (e.g., water and habitat quality)" [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 12–13]. The proportion of the tarpon angling population reached is unknown, few or no respondents came from the Caribbean, Central America, South America, and Africa despite existing fisheries there, and the English-only format makes the effect on response rate "difficult to know" [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 13]. Recall bias may also have affected the decadal and trend questions [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 13].

## Methods and approaches

**Retention and recoding.** Only respondents who completed the entire survey were retained [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3]. Write-in habitats were recoded: "jetties" became nearshore, "mangrove creeks" became estuaries, and two new categories were created for canals, docks, and boat ramps and for coastal or open ocean fishing 3–5 mi offshore [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3]. All analyses were run in R through RStudio, and Likert responses were visualized with the HH package [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3].

**Likert scales.** Overall change in quality used a five-point scale from 1 "Dramatically declined" to 5 "Greatly improved"; decadal quality from 1 "Very poor" to 5 "Very good" with a not-applicable option; threats from 1 "Very low threat" to 5 "Very high threat"; and conservation support and responsibility on matching five-point scales [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 2–3]. Migration timing offered three options: earlier arrival, later arrival, or no change [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 2].

**Ordinal regression.** Ordinal responses (quality change, migration timing, shark losses, and shark loss trend) were modelled with ordinal logistic regression in the ordinal package, which the authors describe as appropriate for Likert data [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3]. Candidate models were built by forward stepwise addition toward a global model and compared by Akaike information criterion with AICcmodavg. The shark global model was:

> "Y Number or Trend = b1 (Guide Status)1 + b2 (Years of Experience)2 + b3 (Yearly Days on the Water)3 + b4 (Angling Location)4 + b5 (Gear Type)5 + b6 (Yearly Number of Atlantic Tarpon Landed)6 + b7 (Fishing Habitat)7 + ε" [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3]

Here Y is the latent continuous variable underlying the ordered response and b the contrast between thresholds [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3]. The migration model used the first four terms. Pairwise contrasts for significant terms used least-square means in emmeans.

**Fit assessment.** Fit was reported as Nagelkerke pseudo R², with the Pulkstenis-Robinson and Hosmer-Lemeshow tests from the generalhoslem package, which the authors identify as the most appropriate of three ordinal goodness-of-fit tests for models with categorical covariates [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3, p. 13]. The quality and migration models returned p < 0.05 on both tests, which "indicates that these models may be missing an independent covariate or an interaction between categorical covariates"; the shark models returned p > 0.05 [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 4, p. 6, p. 8, p. 13]. A threshold of p < 0.05 was justified on the basis of sample size (n > 400) [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 13].

**Decadal and group tests.** For the decadal analysis, respondents fishing since the 1970s were selected, the five-point scale was converted to numbers, and an overall change was calculated per individual [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3]. Two-sample t-tests compared guides with anglers and the 1970s with the 2010s, and Mann-Whitney U tests compared guides with anglers and fly with spin respondents for each decade, threat, and conservation item [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3].

**Binned loss estimates.** Shark losses were estimated from guides only, because guides "likely spend the greatest amount of time fishing" and because charter anglers could double count the same fish [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3]. Each bin received a low and high value, for example 1 and 4 for "1–4 Atlantic tarpon lost," and 20 and 25 for "20 +," and the gear-specific mean of low and high values gave the per-guide range [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3].

**Identity and habitat ranks.** Identity ratings were reduced by principal components analysis with an oblimin oblique rotation and post hoc Cronbach's alpha (minimum α = 0.6) in the psych package; environmentalist, conservationist, and catch-and-release angler combined into a conservation identity (α = 0.68), while hunter, outdoorsperson, and angler stayed separate [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3, p. 6]. Habitat risk rankings were fitted with a Plackett-Luce model in the PlackettLuce package across five options: bridges within 100 yards (about 90 m), passes and inlets, nearshore, rivers and estuaries, and bays and backcountry [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 3].

**Data access.** The survey data are confidential [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 13].

## Open questions

- Whether perceived declines match abundance remains untested, because biological reference points for tarpon are largely unknown; the authors recommend sonar-acoustic surveys of pre-spawning aggregations such as Boca Grande Pass and Bahia Honda [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 9].
- Regional trends outside the United States are poorly represented; a Spanish-language survey and targeted distribution in the Caribbean, Central America, and South America would test whether the patterns hold there [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 13].
- The authors recommend "regular re-administration of surveys like the one used in the current study" to detect regional trends [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 9]. A repeated design would also separate real change from recall bias.
- Survey-based loss counts miss delayed post-release predation, so the gap between angler-reported losses and telemetry-based depredation estimates is unresolved [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 12].
- The poor fit of the quality and migration models points to missing covariates or interactions among categorical covariates that a larger sample might resolve [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 13].
- Gear modifications that shorten fight times and shark deterrent technologies were proposed as "first steps" for depredation but have not been evaluated in this fishery [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 12].

## Connections

- **Informs:** [[post-release-survival-design]]. Angler-reported losses give a stakeholder-side estimate of shark depredation that telemetry studies can test; the survey ranks passes and bridges as the highest-risk habitats, which identifies where acoustic survival studies of hooked tarpon are most needed.
- **Related to:** [[migratory-contingents-and-entrainment]]. Tarpon migrate across state and national boundaries under regulations that range from no harvest limit to catch-and-release only, which is why the authors argue for expanding catch-and-release only regulations across the range [Griffin et al. 2023, p. 10]. Perceived shifts in migration timing are a second line of evidence for the seasonal movements that contingent structure describes.
- **Related to:** predator-prey landscapes of large sharks and game fishes in the Florida Keys (article not yet written). Spatial overlap of sharks and tarpon aggregations would explain why respondents rank passes as the highest-risk habitat.
- **Related to:** tarpon seasonal phenology in the Florida Keys (article not yet written). Telemetry-derived arrival dates offer an independent check on the reported earlier migration among experienced anglers.
