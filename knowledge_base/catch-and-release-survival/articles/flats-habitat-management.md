# Flats habitat management

## Summary

Griffin et al. (2025) argue that catch-and-release practice alone cannot sustain the recreational fisheries for bonefish (*Albula* spp.), permit (*Trachinotus* spp.), and Atlantic Tarpon (*Megalops atlanticus*), because the shallow nearshore habitats these fishes depend on are being lost and degraded with little coordinated protection or restoration. The paper is a synthesis published as a feature in *Fisheries*; it reports no new data and states that "No new data were generated or analysed in support of this research" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 294]. Its contribution is a set of ten guiding principles for bringing habitat management and restoration into flats conservation, illustrated with case material from the Florida Keys, South Florida, the Bahamas, Belize and Mexico, Seychelles, Queensland, Shark Bay, and the Columbia River estuary [Griffin et al. 2025, pp. 287–293]. Every number in the paper is cited from another study, and this article names that study each time.

The paper defines flats as "shallow intertidal areas of the marine environment predominantly located nearshore, spanning from the shoreline to open coastal waters, and are also around isolated atolls" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 288]. The habitat mosaic includes sand, mud, and coral rubble substrates alongside seagrass meadows, oyster reefs, adjacent coral reefs, and mangroves [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 288]. The stated objective is "to identify a comprehensive set of guiding principles (Figure 1) necessary to incorporate habitat management and restoration into the conservation of flats ecosystems and the fishes and fisheries they support" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 289].

## Key points

### Core argument

The case for habitat action rests on three observations. Flats-dependent species are data poor: "With only limited information available on their demographics and population statuses, effective management of flats-dependent species is difficult due to often being categorized as data poor" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 288]. Formal stock assessments are absent, so the authors argue that "it is imperative to restore and maintain the diverse mosaic of flats habitats, which should help to support habitat function, connectivity, and integrity" [Adams & Cooke 2015, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 289]. Habitat protection is also presented as precautionary. The cause of the bonefish recovery in the Florida Keys after its collapse remains unknown and "could also be tied to recruitment or fishing effort", yet "prioritizing habitat improvements and protection offers a precautionary approach to safeguard these fragile nearshore ecosystems" [Boucek et al. 2024, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 289].

The closing position is direct: "decisive action is needed through policy and practice to conserve flats ecosystems, their biodiversity, the fisheries they support and the ecosystem services they provide" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293].

### Habitat extent and loss

Flats and associated habitats cover less than half a percent of the ocean surface but account for 50% of carbon burial in marine sediments [Duarte et al. 2005, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 288]. Between 2014 and 2016, 68% of tidal flats experienced moderate to very high levels of human pressure, including infilling, coastal hardening, vegetation loss, reduced sediment flow, and increased nutrient loads [Hill et al. 2021, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 288]. Tidal flats in North America, East Asia, and the Middle East declined by approximately 16% between 1984 and 2016 [Murray et al. 2019, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 288].

Protection lags behind loss. Only 18% of zones where mangrove, seagrass, and reef communities co-occur fall within protected areas [R. R. Carlson et al. 2021, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290]. Protection covers 16.6% of land and inland water ecosystems but only 7.7% of oceans [Bingham et al. 2021, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 294]. Only 55% of marine spatial plans claim to address land–sea integration [Ehler 2021, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290].

### Fishery value

The catch-and-release fishery for bonefish, Atlantic Tarpon, and permit contributed US$474 million annually to the Florida Keys economy in 2012 [M. Smith et al. 2022, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 289]. Using tournament data, angler logbooks, and local ecological knowledge surveys, a two-fold increase in bonefish catch rates since 2015 was reported, "reflecting a potential increase in populations" [Boucek et al. 2023, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 288]. That increase coincided with an archipelago-wide transition from septic to central sewer systems, which may have improved water quality and supported juvenile bonefish recruitment and survival; the authors state that empirical evidence for this link is still needed [Boucek et al. 2023, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, pp. 288–289].

### Ten guiding principles

Figure 1 lists "Ten guiding principles to integrate habitat management and restoration for the conservation of flats ecosystems" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 289]. In the paper's order they are: embrace flats fishes as flagship and umbrella species; consider connectivity; insert habitat protection and restoration into development and planning processes; learn from habitat management and restoration in other realms; engage in effective partnerships; build the evidence base; think and act at the systems level; embrace transdisciplinary perspectives; emulate nature; and future-proof restoration [Griffin et al. 2025, pp. 289–293].

### Flagship and umbrella species

The authors write that "Like Common Snook, recreational flats species should be recognized as flagship species" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 289]. Protecting Common Snook nursery habitats, including mangrove creeks and wetlands, supports the conservation of 55 other co-occurring species [Wilson et al. 2023, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 289]. Bonefish and Atlantic Tarpon have been suggested as umbrella species candidates [Boucek et al. 2019; Perez et al. 2020, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290]. Permit obtain a median of 70% of their prey from seagrass assemblages despite frequent movements between the Florida reef tract and seagrass flats, a result from stable isotope analysis combined with acoustic telemetry [Brownscombe et al. 2022b, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290]. Protecting mangrove and seagrass habitats for permit would, in turn, support nursery habitats for artisanal and commercial fisheries [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290].

### Connectivity

Adult Atlantic Tarpon undertake seasonal migrations, "sometimes thousands of kilometers", that expose them to a range of habitat and fisheries-related stressors [Griffin et al. 2023a; Luo et al. 2020, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290]. Habitat management for such species cannot stop at a single flat. A ridge-to-reef approach extends restoration "beyond focal habitats" to the watershed hydrology that supports nearshore environments [J. K. Carlson et al. 2019, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290].

### Planning integration

Restoration is costly, so proactive protection is favoured: "Considering the high costs of marine habitat restoration to return their functional roles (Bayraktarov et al., 2016), proactive protection of these ecosystems is essential" [Bayraktarov et al. 2016; Y. Zeng et al. 2022, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290]. Restoring natural freshwater flows from the Florida Everglades could re-establish historic salinity and reduce pollutant effluents, improving habitat quality for bonefish in South Florida [Guardo & Tomasello 1995; Sklar et al. 2005; Brownscombe et al. 2019b, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290].

### Lessons from elsewhere

The paper draws on restoration programmes outside flats. Ducks Unlimited, with 684,000 members in 2023, has contributed to the conservation of over 16 million acres of waterfowl habitat since 1937 [Ducks Unlimited 2023, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 291]. In the Columbia River estuary, 77 restoration projects implemented iteratively for juvenile salmonids restored 7,000 acres of floodplain habitat [Littles et al. 2022, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 291]. Three United States partnership case studies cover oyster restoration in Chesapeake Bay, tidal marsh restoration in San Francisco Bay, and seagrass restoration in Tampa Bay [DeAngelis et al. 2020, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 291]. The authors argue that flats anglers, engaged through habitat-dependent recreation and conservation organisations, "could similarly drive positive policy changes for flats habitat management and restoration" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 291].

### Partnerships

Resource users' involvement and local ecological knowledge led to a rapid policy change on harvest closures for permit at spawning sites [Brownscombe et al. 2019a, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 291]. Partners are most effective when included in management design, implementation, and evaluation, so "partners should be brought in early on when projects are being considered" [G. Smith 2018, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 291].

### Evidence base

Evidence on restoration outcomes for flats is thin: "for flats that are home to flats fishes, such empirical evidence on restoration effectiveness is scant" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292]. Stream restoration offers a contrast, with hundreds of studies on fish biomass and abundance that permit meta-analysis [Foote et al. 2020; Whiteway et al. 2010, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 291]. Reporting bias toward successful outcomes is pervasive [Catalano et al. 2019; Eger et al. 2022, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292], and much restoration work happens outside formal research, so practitioner experience needs to be captured [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292].

### Systems-level predation

This principle ties habitat directly to catch-and-release outcomes. The paper gives no numeric post-release survival or predation rates; it cites the studies qualitatively. Post-release predation of bonefish is "highly variable" and in some instances "may be more influenced by existing predator burden in the angling habitat than angler handling practices", such as air exposure [Cooke & Philipp 2004; Danylchuk et al. 2007a, 2007b; Lennox et al. 2017; Moxham et al. 2019, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292]. Bonefish use deeper water after release [Danylchuk et al. 2007b, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292], which may help them avoid visual predators such as Great Barracuda (*Sphyraena barracuda*) on the flats [Brownscombe et al. 2019c, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292]. Atlantic Tarpon in the Florida Keys recreational fishery are regularly depredated by Great Hammerhead (*Sphyrna mokarran*), a protected species in Florida state waters [Casselberry et al. 2024, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292]. Predator density and movements vary across the seascape and shape both natural and fishery-induced mortality [Griffin et al. 2022, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292].

Controlling predators is not an option when the predators are protected, as with marine mammals and some sharks [R. R. Carlson et al. 2019, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292]. Habitat design is the alternative. The authors propose that "incorporating access to deeper water into flats restoration design may increase survival rates for economically important catch-and-release fisheries like bonefish by reducing post-release predation" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292], and that restoring mangrove and seagrass ecosystems offers "another important strategy for reducing natural and fishery-induced predation risk" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292]. In Queensland, predation rates were highest when restored oyster reefs were surrounded by non-vegetated seafloor far from seagrass and mangrove habitats [Duncan et al. 2019, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292].

### Transdisciplinary work

"Restoration activities fail too often (Suding, 2011) such that there is much need to improve effectiveness" [Suding 2011, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293]. The abstract frames the full set of principles as integrating diverse partnerships, scientific research, legislative initiatives, and local and traditional ecological knowledge [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 288].

### Emulating nature

Existing stressors should be prevented and mitigated "before and during restoration, as this is often the most effective way to facilitate natural recovery" [Elliott et al. 2007, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293]. Interventions can backfire. Structures such as SmartGates help tidal regimes but "can inadvertently create barriers that disrupt the movement of fish and marine megafauna" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293], and artificial reefs can act as fish attractants that increase susceptibility to harvest without promoting long-term population growth [Bohnsack 1998, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293].

### Future-proofing

"Static restoration strategies will likely fall short of long-term restoration objectives" [Harris et al. 2006, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293]. Mangrove planting should be sited using sea level rise projections and storm disturbance probabilities, with tactics such as breakwaters to improve survival and growth [Fillyaw et al. 2021, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293]. The published sentence on adapting to altered precipitation and intensifying storms carries an empty citation parenthetical [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293].

### Policy recommendations

The synthesis concludes that "embracing an ecosystem-based management approach is essential" and that policies are needed "that simplify and expedite processes and effectively incorporate conservation and restoration efforts into a unified legislative strategy" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 294]. Marine protected areas "have shown great potential for substantial conservation outcomes when protections extend to habitats and not just fish" [Gaines et al. 2010, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 294]. Catch-and-release species can act as conservation surrogates alongside no-take areas, "provided that mortality, whether through depredation or post-release, remains low and bycatch is minimized" [Cooke et al. 2006, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 294]. The paper places these recommendations within the United Nations Decade on Ecosystem Restoration, 2021–2030 [United Nations 2019, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 294].

## Methods and approaches

The paper has no methods section, but it names the approaches it expects flats restoration studies to use. These are the parts a lab member would carry into a restoration or habitat-protection project.

- **Monitoring tools.** Electronic tagging [Brownscombe et al. 2022a; Gahagan & Bailey 2020; Lapointe et al. 2013; Piczak et al. 2024, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292] and the integration of ecological knowledge [Mamun 2010; Uprety et al. 2012, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292] are recommended for evaluating flats restoration. Stable isotope analysis and environmental DNA are named as tools for "establishing ecological connections, monitoring biodiversity, and establishing habitat linkages" [Loch & Cook 2023; Wozniak et al. 2006; Capurso et al. 2023; Wee et al. 2023, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292].
- **Experimental design.** Restoration studies should incorporate "appropriate reference sites, replication, and long-term monitoring" [Cooke et al. 2019, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292].
- **Standard reporting.** "A standardized marine restoration monitoring framework is necessary to ensure that detailed reporting contributes to the evidence base" [Eger et al. 2022, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, pp. 291–292]. Unsuccessful outcomes belong in that reporting, given the bias toward success.
- **Adaptive management.** New information is "iteratively incorporated into decision making and management to reduce uncertainty" [Williams & Brown 2014, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 291].
- **Spatial data on human use.** In the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, spatial data on fishing effort and recreational boating inform revisions to spatial management [Black et al. 2015; Anderson 2022; Boucek et al. 2024, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 290].
- **Sediment design rule.** "Restoration designs should emulate natural flats by encouraging silt accumulation where the wave energy is low, slope is gentle, and hydrological connectivity is maintained" [Ganju 2019; Lee et al. 1998; Li et al. 2021, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293].
- **Seagrass prop-scar repair.** Bird stakes placed over prop scars accelerate recovery through nutrient fluxes from bird feces [Kenworthy et al. 2018, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 293].
- **Predation-aware siting.** Restored structures should sit close to vegetated habitat and, for catch-and-release species, give access to deeper water as a post-release refuge [Duncan et al. 2019, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292; Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292].
- **Partner timing.** Partners join at project scoping and stay through design, implementation, and evaluation [G. Smith 2018, cited in Griffin et al. 2025, p. 291].

## Open questions

- Causal links between habitat change and flats fish population trends remain untested. The Florida Keys bonefish recovery coincides with the sewer transition, but recruitment and fishing effort are alternative explanations [Griffin et al. 2025, pp. 288–289].
- Restoration effectiveness for flats fishes has almost no empirical base, and the authors offer no metric or endpoint equivalent to the fish biomass and abundance endpoints used in stream restoration [Griffin et al. 2025, pp. 291–292].
- Whether deeper-water access in restoration design measurably lowers post-release predation of bonefish is proposed but not tested [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292].
- Conflicts between flats fisheries and protected predators, such as Great Hammerhead depredation of tarpon, have no management solution in the paper beyond habitat complexity and refuge [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 292].
- The paper acknowledges "the uncertainties and complexities inherent in ecosystem management" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 294] and leaves open how the ten principles should be prioritised when funding or jurisdiction allows only some of them.
