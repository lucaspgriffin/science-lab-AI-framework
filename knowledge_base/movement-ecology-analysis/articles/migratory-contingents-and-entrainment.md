# Tarpon migratory contingents

## Summary

The entrainment hypothesis holds that migratory routes are learned rather than inherited. In
the words of Griffin et al. (2025, *Fish and Fisheries*), it "posits that migratory routes are
socially transmitted from experienced repeat spawners to younger conspecifics, resulting in
distinct contingents within a population" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733]. The authors evaluated
five biological hypotheses on entrainment, habitat conservatism, and phenotypic plasticity in
Atlantic tarpon (*Megalops atlanticus*), drawing on literature review, life-history data,
mark-and-recapture records, and electronic tagging [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733]. Three Western
Atlantic contingents are the best understood, and movement and recapture data show them to be
spatially discrete with some intermixing. Documented vagrancy suggests that straying adults may
colonise new habitat and may help stocks recover after collective memory of migratory routes is
lost. The synthesis argues for coordinated management across contingent boundaries.

## Key points

### Entrainment

Under entrainment, contingent structure arises from social transmission of routes between age
classes [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733]. The abstract frames the loss of that transmitted knowledge
as "collective memory loss in migratory routes" and notes that straying by a subset of individuals
"may also increase the recovery and resilience of Atlantic tarpon stocks" after such a loss
[Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733]. Management that maintains migratory knowledge is therefore a stated
goal of the synthesis rather than an afterthought.

### Western Atlantic contingents

Griffin et al. (2025) identify the three most well understood Western Atlantic contingents as
follows [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 735]:

1. **Western Gulf of Mexico contingent**
2. **Eastern Gulf of Mexico contingent**
3. **Eastern US Seaboard contingent**

The source states that "The Western and Eastern GOM contingents are believed to overlap but divide
near the Mississippi Delta in Louisiana (Stephens et al. 2024), while the Eastern GOM and Eastern
US Seaboard contingents overlap in South Florida during spawning and overwintering months
(Griffin, et al. 2023)" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 735]. The paper treats these three as the best
characterised of "multiple contingents" thought to exist in Western Atlantic populations
[Griffin et al. 2025, p. 735].

### Life-history stages

The spatially explicit life cycle that the synthesis builds on runs as follows.

- **Larval stage:** the planktonic leptocephalus stage lasts 20–40 days (Shenker et al. 2002),
  after which post-metamorphic juveniles recruit to nearshore areas [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 735].
- **Juvenile nursery residency:** "Juveniles remain in these low-oxygen back-water areas typically
  until they reach ~30 cm fork length (~1 year old) or sometimes until ~60 cm fork length (~2 years
  old) if recruited to ephemerally connected sites" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 735].
- **Shift to the coast:** larger juveniles move towards estuarine habitats and begin to shift to
  exposed coastal regions "at around 10.5 years and a size of 125 cm fork length" (Crabtree et al.
  1997; Kurth et al. 2019) [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 735].
- **Thermal limit:** "juvenile Atlantic tarpon have a mean minimum lethal temperature of 13.7°C"
  (Mace et al. 2017) [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 736].
- **Migratory cue:** adults "are believed to use 26°C isotherms as migratory cues" (Luo et al.
  2020) [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737].

### Behavioural conservatism

Evidence for conservatism comes from a multi-year acoustic telemetry dataset (Griffin et al.
2023). Large juveniles "were generally resident or nomadic until nearing maturity, at which point
they began to adopt adult-like migratory behaviours with high levels of repeatability, that is,
conservatism" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737]. These fish showed seasonal migrations that matched
adults in space and time, "appearing to migrate with repeat spawners to and from pre-spawning
aggregation sites or to putative foraging areas" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737].

Body size did not explain the pattern: "Among migrants, there was no relationship between size and
migratory behaviour" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737]. Individuals showed high intra-individual
variability and high inter-individual repeatability in migratory strategies, which the authors
read as a sign that movement strategies "may not be solely driven or shaped by environmental cues
or swimming capacity, but by social facilitation or learning" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737].

### Vagrancy

Adult tarpon have been documented well outside their typical range. The source reports that
"reports and tracking data have substantiated that adults are capable of vagrancy", including in
"the cooler, temperate waters off Nova Scotia, the Azores and across northwest Europe, such as
Ireland, France, Spain and Portugal" (Twomey and Byrne 1985; Bañón et al. 2019; Luo et al. 2020)
[Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737].

The proposed mechanism links vagrancy to the thermal cue. Because adults appear to follow 26°C
isotherms, "oceanic features associated with the Gulf Stream (e.g., eddies, altered currents) may
occasionally interrupt entrained movements and lead to vagrancy" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737].
The abstract adds that vagrant movements "may contribute to colonising new habitats under suitable
conditions" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733].

### Fishery collapses

Port Aransas, Texas, was labelled the "Tarpon Capital of the World" in the 1920s, and the fishery
peaked in the 1930s [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 736]. By the 1960s it had collapsed, "a decline often
linked to recruitment failure caused by degraded nursery habitats due to freshwater diversions,
prey base depletion and water quality issues" (Holt et al. 2005; Stilwell 2011) [Griffin et al.
2025, p. 736].

The Texas case sits within wider declines. Global historical landings "declined by > 80% between
1965 and 2007" (Adams et al. 2014) [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 736]. In Colombia, the commercial
fishery at a single port "approached nearly 600 t annually in the 1960s before collapsing
completely" (Garcia and Solano 1995) [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 736].

### Management implications

- Contingents are spatially discrete but intermix, so management confined to one region can miss
  part of a contingent's range [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733, p. 735].
- The abstract calls for "coordinated management and conservation efforts across contingent
  boundaries" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733].
- Research on contingent structure, larval recruitment patterns, and abundance trends is named as
  the basis for measures that "ensure the maintenance of migratory knowledge" [Griffin et al. 2025,
  p. 733].

## Methods and approaches

### Hypothesis framework

The synthesis is organised around five biological hypotheses, each stated in the Introduction and
assessed in its own section, with the strength of evidence classed as consistent, supportive, or
demonstrative [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 735].

| No. | Hypothesis | Evidence class | Assessed in |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Spatial and temporal overlap between juveniles and adults | Consistent | §2.1 |
| 2 | Uniform recovery of all spawning units after collapse | Consistent | §2.2 |
| 3 | Identity of colonisers (juvenile or adult vagrants) | Consistent | §2.3 |
| 4 | Juvenile behaviour with and without adults present | Supportive | §2.4 |
| 5 | Contingent overlap or crossing in adult recapture data | Demonstrative | §2.5 |

### Evidence streams

- **Literature review:** Google Scholar searches with keyword sets specific to each hypothesis.
- **Life-history synthesis:** published larval duration, nursery residency, ontogenetic habitat
  shifts, and thermal tolerance values, assembled into a spatially explicit life cycle.
- **Mark-and-recapture records:** the National Marine Fisheries Service Cooperative Tagging Center
  Mark-and-Recapture Program, used to test contingent overlap and crossing in adults
  [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737].
- **Electronic tagging:** multi-year acoustic telemetry of large juveniles, used to
  quantify residency, nomadism, migration, and repeatability of individual strategies
  [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737].

### Reuse for other species

The authors present the approach as "a framework for understanding Atlantic tarpon and other
migratory marine fish stock dynamics" [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733]. A reusable version pairs each
hypothesis with the data stream able to test it: life-history data for juvenile and adult overlap,
landings and abundance histories for recovery, vagrancy records and thermal cues for colonisation,
telemetry repeatability for social learning, and conventional tag recaptures for contingent
crossing. Classing each result as consistent, supportive, or demonstrative keeps the strength of
inference explicit when evidence is indirect.

## Open questions

- **Contingent structure:** the number and boundaries of contingents beyond the three best
  understood remain unresolved, and the source names contingent structure as a research need
  [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733, p. 735].
- **Larval recruitment:** recruitment patterns are named as a research need, which matters because
  the Texas collapse is attributed to recruitment failure [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733, p. 736].
- **Abundance trends:** contingent-level abundance trends are needed to judge whether spawning
  units recover at different rates [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 733].
- **Mixing areas:** the extent and timing of overlap near the Mississippi Delta and in South
  Florida are described qualitatively rather than measured [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 735].
- **Vagrancy frequency:** vagrancy is documented from individual records, and the rate at which
  adults stray, and whether strays establish, is not quantified [Griffin et al. 2025, p. 737].
