# Snook range expansion

## Summary

Common snook (*Centropomus undecimalis*) are a tropical, cold-sensitive sport fish whose historical range in the northeastern Gulf of Mexico stopped south of Cedar Key, Florida. Purtlebaugh et al. (2020) used 22 years (1997–2018) of Florida Fisheries-Independent Monitoring (FIM) catches in the Suwannee River estuary to document a poleward expansion: the first snook in the program data was a single adult in 2000, yearly occurrences began in 2007, and catches rose exponentially from 2012 through 2018, when 231 fish were caught, the highest of the series [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7]. Fish smaller than 100 mm standard length (SL) were present by 2018, which the authors read as evidence of local reproduction [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8]. The number of days per year below the 12 degree C lower lethal limit at the Cedar Key air-temperature gauge declined over the same period [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7].

The expansion is a case in which a long-term, standardised survey designed for other purposes detected a range shift without any change in design. The same program later added dedicated juvenile snook sampling in 2016 in three other estuaries, because stock-assessment analysts needed better data on fish of 100–300 mm SL [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]. A severe cold event in January 2010 is visible in FIM data as a sharp decline and a recovery within roughly three to four years, both at the range edge and in southern estuaries [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 13; FWRI 2023, slide 23].

## Key points

### Catch trajectory

Purtlebaugh et al. (2020) pooled catches across all gears each year for the Suwannee River estuary and the Cedar Key area. In total, 625 snook ranging from 53 mm to 885 mm SL were collected [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7]. The sequence of events reported was:

- **1997 and 1998:** no snook in the 183-m haul seine (95 and 120 hauls) [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6].
- **2000:** one snook (703 mm SL) captured near the Cedar Key islands, documenting the northernmost extent of the range in program data [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7].
- **2007:** "Another snook was not captured again until 2007, when yearly occurrences of snook began to appear during sampling" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7].
- **2012 through 2018:** "there was an exponential increase in snook catches" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7].
- **2016 to 2018:** the catch in 2016 was 56 fish, in 2017 it was 163 ("tripled catches from the previous year"), and in 2018 it was 231 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7].

The paper does not tabulate yearly counts for 2007 to 2015; those values appear only in its Figure 4 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8].

### Exponential fit

The yearly catch series was fitted to an exponential model, C = a × exp^(b × Year), by maximum likelihood with a lognormal error distribution, where a is the intercept parameter and b is the exponent parameter [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 5]. It was compared with an intercept-only model (constant catch through time) using Akaike information criterion, with the rule that values greater than 10 have negligible credibility relative to the lowest-AIC model [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6]. The intercept-only model had a delta AIC of 188, "indicating that the constant catch through time model had near zero credibility relative to the exponential model" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7]. The fitted values of a and b are not reported, and the paper does not say how years with zero catch were handled in the lognormal fit [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 5–6]. Software and package names are not given.

### Gears and effort

The FIM program sampled the Suwannee River estuary monthly with a stratified-random design, using three gears: a 183-m haul seine, a 21.3-m seine, and a 6.1-m otter trawl [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. The estuary was divided into zones and a grid of cells of 1-min latitude by 1-min longitude, and cells were randomly selected for sampling and stratified by habitat and depth [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. Effort and sampling locations were nearly constant over 1997–2018 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4].

| Gear and stratum | Hauls per year | Years | Snook caught | Share of total catch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 183 × 2.5-m haul seine, bay | 192 | 1999–2018 | 610 | 97.6% |
| 21.3 × 1.8-m seine, bay | 252 | 1998–2018 | 1 | 0.2% |
| 21.3 × 1.8-m seine, tidal creeks | 108 | 1998–2018 | 10 | 1.6% |
| 21.3 × 1.8-m seine, Suwannee River | 60 | 2002–2018 | 3 | 0.5% |
| 6.1-m otter trawl, Suwannee River | 60 | 2002–2018 | 1 | 0.2% |

All values are from Table 1 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6]. The haul seine was fished 95 times in 1997 and 120 times in 1998 before reaching 192 hauls per year, and the Lower Suwannee River was added to the sampling universe in 2001 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4, p. 6]. These changes preceded the appearance of snook in the data, apart from the single fish in 2000 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4].

The haul seine targets subadult and adult fish along shorelines in water of 2.5 m or less [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. The 21.3-m seine fishes shallow water (1.8 m or less along shorelines, 1.5 m or less in open water) and the otter trawl fishes 1.8–7.6 m, and both target age-0 and juvenile fish [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. Snook caught by those two gears came almost exclusively (93%) from tidal creek and river habitat [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7].

The text reports the haul seine share as 98%, the Methods give "greater than 97%", and Table 1 gives 97.6%; these agree within rounding [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4, p. 6, p. 7]. Because haul-seine effort was constant from 1999 to 2018 and the haul seine caught nearly all snook, the authors summed catches over gears and used raw annual counts. No catch-per-unit-effort standardisation was applied [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4–5].

### Spatial and size spread

Spatial structure supports the interpretation that the increase was a real expansion and not a few large catches at a few sites [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8, p. 11].

- Fish caught in 2007–2011 came primarily from around the Cedar Key islands [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8].
- In 2012–2015 the distribution extended around the islands and north toward the Suwannee River [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8].
- In 2016–2018 snook were caught north of the Suwannee River, in the river (four fish), and in tidal creeks (10 fish) [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8].
- Frequency of occurrence at haul-seine sites rose through the series, which the authors take to show that the exponential increase in 2016–2018 "reflected an expansion in spatial distribution over time" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 11].

For reproduction, the Results state that by 2018 "a population that appeared fully established with presumed local reproduction ongoing with fish less than 100 mm SL" was present [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8]. The Discussion places the first age-0 fish in 2010, followed by juveniles in later years, and says that age-0 and juvenile snook were commonly observed in tidal creeks from 2016 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 13]. The abstract refers to strong evidence of local reproduction during 2016–2018 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 1]. The first age-0 year of 2010 and the 2016–2018 window are reported in different sections and are not reconciled in the paper.

### Cold-day trend

Purtlebaugh et al. (2020) used air temperature from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) gauge CDRF1 at Cedar Key, because a long water-temperature series had data gaps. They justified air temperature on the ground that water in the sampling region is shallower than 2 m, so water and air temperatures are likely to be similar [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. The index was the number of days per year on which temperature reached below 12 degrees C, "the lower lethal limit for snook", regressed on year for 18 years from 2000, the year of the first snook in the program data [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. The number of such days "has significantly (F1,17 = 5.72, P<0.05) declined since 2000" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 7]. The Figure 3 caption calls the gauge a "buoy" while the Methods call it a gauge [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4, p. 7].

The authors give the rationale for a fixed gauge: fixed measurements at a stationary location reduce variability, support consistent comparison, and had fewer missing points than other sources [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. They also note that less frequent but more intense cold events remain possible, for example through polar vortexes, and that a newly established population is still likely to meet cold events greater in magnitude or duration than those in the historical range [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 11, p. 12].

### Low-temperature captures

Snook were caught in every month, and were least abundant (N = 5) in January and February, when mean water temperatures were 14.4 and 15.8 degrees C, respectively [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8]. Catches rose in March and April (N = 90) as water approached 20 degrees C, fell in May (N = 25), reached N = 207 over June–August, peaked at N = 210 in September and October, and were N = 88 in November and December [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8]. These monthly totals sum to 625.

During a cold event in December 2017, when water temperature fell below 12 degrees C, two snook were caught at 11.9 degrees C and five at 13.5 degrees C. Another 19 were caught at 13.9 degrees C in December 2018 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8–9]. The paper describes the 11.9 degree C captures as "below the reported lethal low temperature of 12.5˚C" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8]. The Methods, however, use 12 degrees C as the lower lethal limit for the air-temperature index [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. Both values appear in the paper and are not reconciled there. The authors also note that the catch drop in May may reflect snook moving to spawning aggregations in deep channels, offshore wrecks, or reefs, outside the reach of the gear [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 14].

### Cold event of 2010

The paper states that snook in Florida had extensive mortality in the winters of 1989–1990, 2000–2001, and 2009–2010, without a citation for that sentence [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 12]. In the FIM series, red mangrove, black mangrove, and snook "all showed a substantial decrease in frequency of occurrence during a hard freeze in 2010, but slightly rebounded the following year and continued to increase" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 11]. Snook catches in the study area "were noticeably reduced by the 2010 cold kill, but fully recovered within three years" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 13]. The paper cites Stevens et al. (2016) for recovery that took as long as four years depending on location [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 13].

The 2023 Florida Forage Fish Research Program workshop slides show the same event from the southern estuaries. Slide 23, titled "Data Use – Cold Event", states "Severe cold event in January 2010" and "FIM monitoring data documented decline and recovery of Common Snook in several estuaries" [FWRI 2023, slide 23]. The panel plots log CPUE of snook of 500 mm SL or larger for Tampa Bay, Charlotte Harbor, Shark River, northern Indian River Lagoon, and southern Indian River Lagoon, 1996–2014, with a dashed line at 2010. Values read from the figure are approximate: Tampa Bay and Charlotte Harbor fall to about 0.17 and 0.07 at 2010 and recover by about 2013 to 2014 [FWRI 2023, slide 23]. The Suwannee series and the southern series therefore agree on a recovery of about three to four years, though the slide gives no statistical summary. Slide 24, "Data Use – Expanding Species Distribution", shows frequency of occurrence of snook, black mangrove, and red mangrove for 1997–2018, with snook near 28% in 2018 (approximate, read from the figure) [FWRI 2023, slide 24]. This is the same series as Purtlebaugh et al. (2020).

### Juvenile survey

The Suwannee record shows what the standard design captures at the range edge: adults in the haul seine and very few juveniles in the smaller gears [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6]. A separate problem arose for stock assessment in the core range. In early 2013 the program was tasked with developing a design to improve collections of juvenile snook for Florida's snook stock assessment, because analysts wanted better estimates of abundance, survival, and growth of snook of 100–300 mm SL for age-based models [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]. Before the change, fish above 300 mm SL were well represented in haul-seine catches and fish below 100 mm SL in 21.3-m center-bag seine catches, "but those of 100–300 mm SL were not as well represented in either gear, especially in the southern estuaries (Charlotte Harbor and Southern Indian River Lagoon)" [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]. The authors give approximate age classes by size: age-0 below 100 mm, age-1 at 100–300 mm, and age-2 and older above 300 mm SL, with several ages present within the older size ranges [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4].

Gear testing ran from July 2014 to June 2015 in Tampa Bay, Charlotte Harbor, and the Indian River Lagoon [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 5]. It collected 1,872 seine samples and 2,315 juvenile snook (1,285 age-0, 714 age-1, and 316 age-2 and older) [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 6]. The 21.3-m seine accounted for more than 95% of the catch, and the experimental 40-m center-bag seine did not improve catch of age-1 and age-2 fish, whose catch per unit effort was similar between the two seines [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 6]. Within rivers, age-0 and juvenile snook were more abundant in backwaters and smaller tidal tributaries than in main-stem areas, and the 21.3-m seine caught juveniles in every month [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 6].

The modified survey began in January 2016. It added monthly 21.3-m seine effort in previously undersampled backwater and tidal tributary water bodies, treated as a new geospatial stratum [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 7]. Added hauls per month were 8 in Tampa Bay, 30 in Charlotte Harbor across 27 tidal creeks, and 24 in the Indian River Lagoon, including the St. Sebastian River and two river systems (St. Lucie and Loxahatchee) not in the standard design [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 7]. These sum to 62 hauls per month and 744 per year, matching the stated annual sample size of n = 744 [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9]. Randomised retention of snook of 100 mm SL or larger for biological samples was also established [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 7]. The design improved indices of abundance of age-0 snook and strengthened age–length keys, and data from the reconnaissance and gear-testing phases were used immediately in snook stock assessments (Munyandorero et al. 2020, cited in Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 8). Funding came from a continuing grant from Florida's Snook Stamp program, and the survey cost less than one third of the funding needed to establish and maintain a new field laboratory [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9].

After five years of level funding, the program revisited the design in 2020 and found that "spatial sampling reductions could not be implemented without affecting data quality and the long-term data sets" [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9]. It compared four temporal scenarios (6, 7, 8, and 9 months) and implemented the 9-month scenario in 2021, with no sampling in March, May, or July, a reduction of 25% (333 annual net hauls) [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9]. The paper does not state the base to which the 25% applies; the quoted 333 hauls imply a base of 1,332 annual hauls, which is a derived value and not a stated one.

### Stated limitations

The authors list several constraints on interpretation.

- The temperature trend uses air temperature at one gauge, whereas the seasonal plot uses water temperature measured at net deployment [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4, p. 6].
- Water-temperature data had gaps that precluded a long-term water series [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4].
- The last extended cold event that killed mangroves extensively in the area was 1989, which the authors give as a personal observation [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 11].
- The paper records counts, not density or biomass, so the exponential fit describes catch and not abundance [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 5].

### Data access

All Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission data and records fall under the Sunshine Law, and the FIM data are available to the public on request to the program [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 1]. The program publishes gear and effort definitions in its metadata, and the 2023 workshop slides note that calculations of effort, length extrapolations, and splitter expansions are documented there [FWRI 2023, slide 27].

## Methods and approaches

**Detecting a range shift from a standing survey.** The workflow in Purtlebaugh et al. (2020) can be reused for any species at a range edge covered by a standardised monitoring program.

1. Confirm that effort and locations were stable. Here, effort for all gears did not change during 2002–2018, and haul-seine effort was constant for 1999–2018 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4–5]. Document any design changes that preceded the first detections.
2. Decide whether gears can be pooled. The authors pooled because one gear took more than 97% of the fish. When one gear dominates, a single-gear analysis is the cleaner alternative, and the paper uses it for frequency of occurrence (haul seine only) [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6].
3. Test the trend against a null model. Exponential and intercept-only models were both fitted by maximum likelihood with a lognormal distribution and compared by delta AIC with a threshold of 10 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 5–6]. Report the fitted parameters, which this paper does not, and state how zero-catch years entered the lognormal fit.
4. Check that growth is spatial and not a few large catches. Map catches by block of years on the program grid, and plot yearly frequency of occurrence at fixed gear sites [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6, p. 11].
5. Record a habitat covariate from the same hauls. Occurrence of mangroves was recorded at each seine haul, giving a frequency-of-occurrence series for black and red mangrove beside the snook series [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6].
6. Use size classes to infer recruitment. Age-0 was defined as below 100 mm SL and juveniles as 100–300 mm SL [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6]. Fish below 100 mm SL are the signal for local reproduction.

**Cold-day index.** For a species with a known lower lethal limit, count days per year below that limit at a fixed gauge and regress the count on year [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. This index is a proxy for winter severity and is not a measure of water temperature at the sampling sites, a limitation the authors accept because of gaps in the water series [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. The seasonal catch plot, in contrast, used the mean water temperature taken at each net deployment by month [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6].

**Adding a stratum without breaking a time series.** Schrandt et al. (2024) added the juvenile snook effort as a new spatial stratum, and note that a stock-assessment model can absorb added samples from a new zone within an existing estuary by including zone as a factor [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 19]. Their design sequence was reconnaissance (haul seines from 9 to 183 m long and meshes from 3.2 to 38.1 mm stretch), a year of paired gear testing stratified by water-body type (main stem, backwater, tidal tributary), then allocation of effort among estuaries based on the gear-test results and existing survey data [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 5, p. 7].

## Open questions

The authors identify the following needs at the range edge [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 1, p. 13–14]:

- **Thermal refuges.** "A key uncertainty is the degree to which thermal refugia in the form of groundwater springs may provide resilience to this snook population and allow individuals to withstand cold kill events." Changes in freshwater flow could change winter refuge availability, which the authors call "a key future research need" with implications for minimum-flow regulations [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 13].
- **Nursery habitat.** Identifying nursery habitat is called a high priority if managers wish to encourage a sustainable recreational snook fishery; they suggest extending monitoring into remote tidal creeks and marsh ponds, and quantifying each nursery's contribution to the adult population [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 14].
- **Biology at the edge.** Growth, mortality, and spawning may differ at higher latitude. The authors expect a shorter spawning season and faster growth in the Suwannee estuary than in the historical range, and note that differences could alter bag or size limits [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 13]. These are expectations stated by the authors and are not tested in the paper.
- **Food web effects.** Snook have high diet overlap with red drum in south Florida, and red drum increased sharply there after the 2010 cold kill of snook [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 14]. The authors suggest the arrival of snook could affect prey populations and the growth of red drum and spotted seatrout, and call for studies of food habits, habitat overlap, and competition [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 1, p. 14].
- **Mangrove link.** Black mangrove frequency rose from 2000 and red mangrove from 2007, but "It is unclear whether the mangrove expansion northward is a mechanism for snook expansion" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 9, p. 12].
- **Source of the first fish.** The authors hypothesise that the early fish immigrated from southern estuaries; no data in the paper test this [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 8].
- **Recruitment variability.** "The variability of recruitment and level of survival are not clear and warrant further investigation" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 13–14].
