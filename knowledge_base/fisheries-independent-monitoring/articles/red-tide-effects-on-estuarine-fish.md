# Red tide effects

## Summary

Red tide, a bloom of the dinoflagellate *Karenia brevis*, is an acute disturbance on the Florida Gulf coast that can reach ichthyotoxic cell concentrations in estuaries. Two sources in the knowledge base address its effects on fish, and they rest on different data. Walters et al. (2013) ran their own passive acoustic hydrophone survey of sand seatrout (*Cynoscion arenarius*) spawning aggregations in Tampa Bay in 2004 and 2005, and found that aggregations in the red tide-exposed lower bay declined from 38 percent of stations to 10 percent of stations in the bloom year, while aggregations in the unaffected upper bay held or increased. That survey is not Fisheries-Independent Monitoring (FIM) data, although it borrowed the FIM sampling universe and grid selection program for its layout. The only FIM data in that paper are a juvenile sand seatrout series, read from a figure, which fell to record lows after the 2005 bloom. The second source is a 2023 FIM workshop presentation that lists red tide among the disturbances addressed with FIM long-term abundance series and with model-estimated mortality for gag (*Mycteroperca microlepis*) and red grouper (*Epinephelus morio*); the slides state these uses but give no mortality estimates.

## Key points

### Source separation

Results in this article come from three distinct data streams, and the distinction governs what each can support.

- **Hydrophone survey (not FIM).** The authors' own passive acoustic survey of sand seatrout spawning aggregations, 2004 and 2005, Tampa Bay [Walters et al. 2013, p. 191]. All zone, year, and monthly percentages below come from this survey.
- **FIM juvenile trawl series (FIM).** Fig. 7 of Walters et al. (2013) reproduces the FIM juvenile sand seatrout series, 1996 to 2011 [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198]. Values from this figure are read from the plot and are approximate.
- **FIM program slides (FIM).** The 2023 workshop slides describe uses of FIM long-term data for red tide, with plotted series read from slide images [FWRI 2023, slide 22]. Values from these plots are also approximate.

### Red tide classes

Walters et al. (2013) obtained *K. brevis* cell counts from the Harmful Algal Bloom group of the Florida Fish and Wildlife Research Institute and collected water samples "approximately weekly from 4 fixed stations near the mouth of Tampa Bay" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 193]. Samples were enumerated at 100× and 400× on an inverted microscope. They were ranked as "background concentration (0 to 1000 cells l−1), bloom concentration (1001 to 100 000 cells l−1), or possible ichthyotoxic conditions (>100 000 cells l−1)" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 193]. The authors note that "cell counts are an approximation of potential lethality" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 193], so the classes describe exposure risk and not measured toxicity to fish.

### Bloom timing

The 2004 spawning season (April to September) had no red tide in Tampa Bay [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195]. A short winter bloom occurred earlier: ichthyotoxic concentrations were detected in 20 percent of samples (n = 80) from lower Tampa Bay in January and February 2004, and none of the remaining samples (n = 52) from March through the end of 2004 contained *K. brevis* cells [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195]. In 2005, the bloom "initiated off the west-central Florida coast in early January 2005" and affected coastal waters through January 2006 [Walters et al. 2013, p. 192]. Background concentrations appeared in the second week of January at stations 2 and 4, all four stations reached bloom levels by mid-January, and ichthyotoxic levels were reached at station 3 on 10 February and at station 4 on 28 February [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195]. By June, 56 percent of samples from the four stations were at ichthyotoxic levels, and this continued through June and July [Walters et al. 2013, p. 196]. Ichthyotoxic levels were continuous in zones 2 and 3 through the summer but "never extended into Zone 1" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 196]. The bloom therefore began about three months into the April to September spawning season and left the upper bay as an unexposed reference area.

### Spawning response

In the hydrophone survey, aggregations were detected at 41 percent of sampled stations in 2004 (351 aggregations at 851 stations) and 33 percent in 2005 (286 aggregations at 864 stations) [Walters et al. 2013, pp. 196–197]. A binomial model showed a significant interaction between zone and year (F2,1709 = 30.45, p < 0.01) [Walters et al. 2013, p. 197]. The zone-level pattern changed as follows.

| Zone | 2004 aggregations (% of stations) | 2005 aggregations (% of stations) |
|---|---|---|
| Zone 1, upper bay | 35 | 46 |
| Zone 2, middle bay | 51 | 49 |
| Zone 3, lower bay | 38 | 10 |

Values are from the hydrophone survey [Walters et al. 2013, p. 197]. In 2004, zone 2 had significantly more aggregations than zones 1 and 3. In 2005, zones 1 and 2 had significantly more aggregations than zone 3. Compared with 2004, "the location (Fig. 5) and frequency (Fig. 4) of aggregations shifted significantly in Zones 1 and 3, but remained static in Zone 2" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 197].

The within-season contrast is sharper. In 2005, more aggregations were detected in the first three months (46 percent) than in the second half (21 percent), "a marked reduction driven by the drop in the number of aggregations in the lower 2 zones coinciding with the arrival of red tide" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 197]. After the bloom reached the lower bay, no aggregations were detected at any sampled station in zone 3 (n = 192), against 42 percent of stations (n = 216) in the same months of 2004 [Walters et al. 2013, p. 197]. Monthly zone 3 values in 2005 were 23 percent in April, 24 percent in May, and 0 percent in July, August, and September [Walters et al. 2013, p. 197, Table 2].

### Spawning inference limits

Walters et al. (2013) concluded that "K. brevis toxicity is likely the principal cause of the reduction in spawning" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 199]. They did not measure mortality. They state that "it is difficult to determine with this methodology alone whether this decline was due to fish movement, cessation of spawning activity, or mortality" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 197]. They also cite the broader difficulty that "unequivocal data that would distinguish between significant and sustained fishery losses attributable to mortality from HAB toxicity and losses attributable to avoidance of an area have been difficult to obtain" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 199]. Hillsborough Bay was not sampled in 2005, so the response of that part of the spawning population is unknown [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198]. Only two years of hydrophone data exist, and only one was a bloom year, so year is confounded with red tide exposure; the authors note the limits of inferring trends without a post-perturbation year (2006) [Walters et al. 2013, p. 200].

The authors also reasoned that the broad spatial spread of aggregations, together with certain reproductive and life history characteristics, "may buffer the population from long-term effects" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 191].

### Juvenile series

The FIM juvenile series in Fig. 7 of Walters et al. (2013) is labelled "Cynoscion arenarius (≤50 mm SL, Apr–Oct, 6.1 m river trawls)" with the y-axis "Number per set", spanning 1996 to 2011 [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198]. The figure shows the median with 25th to 75th percentile boxes and 10th to 90th percentile error bars. The paper gives no standardisation model and does not describe how zero catches were handled; the analysis is credited to C. Guenther in the caption [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198].

Read from the figure (approximate), the median was about 17 per set around 2000, about 8 to 9 per set in 1997 to 1999 and 2002 to 2003, about 6.5 per set in 2004, about 1 to 2 per set in 2005 to 2007, and under 1 in 2008. It recovered to about 4 per set in 2009 and 2010 and about 8 per set in 2011 [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198]. The authors state that annual relative abundance "remained at record low numbers until 2009" and refer to "the 4 yr depression in juvenile abundance" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198]. They cite a "significant decline in relative abundance of juvenile sand seatrout from the rivers leading into the estuary during 2005 and 2006" from Flaherty and Landsberg (2011); that decline is a result of the cited paper, not of Walters et al. [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198].

The hydrophone and juvenile data streams connect in the discussion of Walters et al. (2013) in two ways. First, juveniles appear in Tampa Bay from April through October [Walters et al. 2013, p. 192], and the three rivers feeding Hillsborough Bay have some of the highest juvenile catch densities in the bay [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198]. Second, the authors state that the 2005 juvenile decline "may have been due to the direct mortality of juveniles or larvae" if they met red tide while moving from higher-salinity bay waters to riverine habitat [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198]. This is an interpretation, not a test, and the juvenile series is a separate FIM dataset from the hydrophone survey.

### FIM program use

The 2023 workshop slides, presented at the Florida Forage Fish Research Program Data Workshop on 24 May 2023, devote one slide to "Potential Impacts of Red Tide" with two stated uses: "Insight from long-term data into change in abundance" and "Model-estimated mortality for Gag, Red Grouper" [FWRI 2023, slide 22]. The slide text gives no mortality values, model structure, or citation for the gag and red grouper estimates, so the estimates are reported here only as a stated use.

The plotted panels on that slide are read from the image and are approximate [FWRI 2023, slide 22]. The left panel shows catch per unit effort (individuals per site) for the Florida Panhandle and the West Florida Shelf, 2014 to 2020, with a red highlight bar at 2018; the Panhandle value is about 18 in 2018 and the West Florida Shelf value about 5. The right panel shows fish per set for Sarasota Bay (2009 to 2020) and Charlotte Harbor (1996 to 2020) with the 2018 bar highlighted, and both fall to near 0.1 in 2018. The slide does not name the species in either panel, so these panels cannot be attributed to a species or compared with the sand seatrout results. They show the data-use pattern: a long-term FIM series with the event year marked.

The slides frame red tide within a wider priority, "Perturbance Impacts (And Recovery!)": "Chronic vs. acute environmental drivers of abundance", with "Red tide, storms, droughts, Lake Okeechobee discharges, septic tanks" as examples and "Spatially explicit temporal models relating perturbance to inshore/offshore datasets" as a proposed approach [FWRI 2023, slide 31].

### Source comparison

The two sources address red tide with different designs. Walters et al. (2013) used a targeted two-year acoustic survey with an unexposed upper bay as a reference, and measured spawning aggregations directly. The FIM slides rely on long-term, standardised abundance series that predate and follow the event [FWRI 2023, slide 8], and report abundance declines and modelled mortality, not spawning behaviour. The one place the data streams meet is the FIM juvenile series for sand seatrout in Tampa Bay, which Walters et al. (2013) use as supporting evidence. The slides do not quantify the declines in text.

## Methods and approaches

### Passive acoustic survey design

The hydrophone survey followed a random stratified design, with Tampa Bay divided into three zones (upper, middle, and lower bay) on geographic and logistical criteria [Walters et al. 2013, p. 193].

- **Grids.** Each zone was composed of 1 nautical mile-square grids. Only grids containing water deeper than 1.5 m in at least 5 percent of the grid area were included (n = 285) [Walters et al. 2013, p. 193]. Grids were chosen by random selection with replacement "to account for seasonal variability" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 193]. The FIM Program shared "their sampling universe design as well as grid selection program" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 200].
- **Season and time.** Sampling ran from the first week of April through the last week of September in 2004 and 2005, from about 20:00 h EDT (sunset) to 01:00 h EDT. These periods were set by spotted seatrout (*Cynoscion nebulosus*) spawning, the original focus of the survey [Walters et al. 2013, p. 193].
- **Schedule.** Sampling occurred twice weekly in a zone, within 2 days of quarter, full, last quarter, or new moon phases. One zone was sampled per week, and zones were rotated monthly so each was sampled over all lunar phases [Walters et al. 2013, p. 193]. Because of the rotation some zone-by-month cells are empty in Table 2 [Walters et al. 2013, p. 197].
- **Effort per night.** Five grids per trip in zone 1 and six grids per trip in zones 2 and 3 [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195].
- **Stations.** Four stations per grid, distributed as evenly as possible over four bottom types: submerged aquatic vegetation, structure, channel, and nonchannel [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195]. If depth was constant within a grid, stations were spaced equidistant [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195].
- **Recording.** A mobile hydrophone (HTI model 96-min, sensitivity −164 dBV µPa−1) was lowered 1 m. After a 2-minute wait, all sciaenid courtship calls were identified and recorded [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195]. Field recordings were cross-referenced against a standard sound reference library for other fish species of interest [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195].
- **Spawning proxy.** The number of sand seatrout producing sound was scored as 1 to 2 individuals, 3 to 5 individuals, or an aggregation, "the category used as the proxy for spawning" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195]. The method rests on male sand seatrout producing a species-specific call associated with reproduction [Walters et al. 2013, p. 192].

### Statistical model

The response was the percentage of stations with aggregations. The authors fitted a generalized linear model with a binomial distribution and maximum likelihood estimation in PROC GLIMMIX of SAS 9.1, with fixed effects of zone, year, and their interaction, and alpha of 0.05 [Walters et al. 2013, p. 195]. Aggregation locations were mapped in ArcGIS 9.2 [Walters et al. 2013, p. 194]. For the within-2005 contrast, Fig. 6 maps April to June as before red tide and July to September as after [Walters et al. 2013, p. 198].

### Reusable design features

A lab member planning a disturbance study can take four features from this design. The grids and strata are drawn from an existing monitoring sampling universe, so effort is spatially even. An unexposed zone acts as an in-year reference. A fixed set of stations near the bay mouth supplies weekly bloom cell counts on a three-class scale. Aggregation percentage is modelled with zone, year, and their interaction, so a change confined to exposed zones appears as an interaction.

The design has no pre-event baseline within the bloom year other than the first three months, and a single non-bloom year. A multi-year baseline, such as the FIM juvenile series, is the means by which the authors say causal and coincidental effects can be separated: "Establishing baseline data, such as the annual juvenile abundance measures, can facilitate determinations of how an animal, community, or ecosystem is affected and if the effects are causal or coincidental" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 200].

### FIM series baseline

The slides describe the features of FIM data that make event analysis possible: a statistical, usually stratified-random sampling design, standardised methods across labs and years, long-term consistency, and broad temporal and spatial coverage [FWRI 2023, slides 8–10]. The program collects water temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and pH [FWRI 2023, slide 15], so environmental context is available when an event year is marked. For the species-level estimation of red tide mortality, the slides do not describe the model, so its structure, data filters, and thresholds must be taken from the primary analysis once located.

## Open questions

- The hydrophone survey cannot separate fish movement, cessation of spawning, and mortality. Walters et al. (2013) propose incorporating circulation models to relate spawning sites, larval transport, and red tide, which "could elucidate the relative contribution of larvae by zone" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 200].
- It is unknown whether the sand seatrout spawning population recovered in 2006 and later, because no post-perturbation hydrophone year exists in the paper [Walters et al. 2013, p. 200].
- The model-estimated mortality for gag and red grouper is named on slide 22 but not described. The primary analysis, model, and values need to be located and extracted before they can be compared with the sand seatrout results [FWRI 2023, slide 22].
- The species shown in the slide 22 CPUE panels is not named. Attribution to a species, and whether the 2018 declines are red tide effects, cannot be assessed from the slides alone [FWRI 2023, slide 22].
- Walters et al. (2013) ask for studies "with comprehensive spatial and temporal components in order to quantify losses to ecosystems" [Walters et al. 2013, p. 200]. Whether FIM and a targeted acoustic survey could be run jointly in the same bay is untested in these sources.
- The authors argue that spatially explicit environmental data in stock assessments would let managers account for perturbations that conventional methods were not designed to address [Walters et al. 2013, p. 200]. How red tide enters FIM-based assessment indices is not described in the sources.

## Connections

- **Related to:** [[cold-stunning-and-ocean-warming]]. Red tide and cold events are both acute disturbances that produce abrupt abundance declines and recoveries. The FIM slides pair them as "Potential Impacts of Red Tide" and "Cold Event" [FWRI 2023, slides 22–23], and cold-stunning provides the contrasting case of a physical, not biotoxic, driver acting on a different taxon.
- **Related to:** [[detection-efficiency-and-range-testing]]. Both the hydrophone survey and acoustic telemetry depend on detecting sound at a sampling point, and in both an absence of detections reflects the combined effect of presence, behaviour, and detection. The hydrophone survey does not estimate detection probability, so zero aggregations in zone 3 are read as absence of calling aggregations at the sampled stations.
- **Informs:** [[fim-survey-design]]. The acoustic survey reused the FIM sampling universe, and the red tide examples depend on FIM standardisation and stratification.
- **Informs:** [[juvenile-indices-and-stock-assessment]]. The juvenile sand seatrout series is the FIM component that links the 2005 bloom to recruitment, and the slides list abundance indices and size and age composition as FIM inputs to assessments [FWRI 2023, slide 21].

- **Related to:** [[forage-fish-dynamics]] and [[freshwater-inflow-and-nekton]]. The slides group red tide with storms, droughts, and Lake Okeechobee discharges as acute drivers of abundance [FWRI 2023, slide 31].
