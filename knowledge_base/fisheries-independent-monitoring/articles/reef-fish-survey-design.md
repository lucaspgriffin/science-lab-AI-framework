# Reef fish survey design

## Summary

The offshore reef fish video survey of the Florida Fish and Wildlife Research Institute (FWRI) samples natural and artificial reefs on the West Florida Shelf with stereo-baited remote underwater video (S-BRUV) under a two-stage stratified random design. Switzer et al. (2023) used classification and regression tree analyses of 2014–2017 video and side-scan habitat data to define three regional strata, three depth strata, and three relief and three size classes of habitat, then allocated a fixed annual effort of 1000 natural reef and 200 artificial reef deployments in proportion to habitat availability and managed-species richness [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 1–2, 8, 10–11]. Simulations of 1000 repeated surveys showed that the resulting design estimated relative abundance of most natural reef species with median bias below 10% and median absolute deviation below 30%, whereas artificial reef estimates were biased and less precise [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 1–2, 13–14]. The design was implemented in 2020 as the Gulf Fishery Independent Survey of Habitat and Ecosystem Resources (G-FISHER) [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 2]. The nursery counterpart is the polyhaline seagrass trawl survey for juvenile reef fish described in [[fim-survey-design]] and [[juvenile-indices-and-stock-assessment]].

## Key points

### Survey frame

The Gulf-wide reef fish video effort rests on three collaborative surveys, which provide the most comprehensive multispecies characterisation of reef fish abundance and size composition in the Gulf of Mexico: the Southeast Area Monitoring and Assessment Program (SEAMAP) survey, the National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) Panama City survey, and the FWRI survey [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 2]. All three use identical S-BRUV arrays [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 2–3]. The FWRI program expanded to offshore habitats in 2008 to address limits in available data on groundfish assemblages [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 3], and the 2008 entry on the program timeline lists side scan sonar, underwater video, a 12.3-m trawl, and hooked gear for the Gulf of Mexico [FWRI 2023, slide 7].

The FWRI natural reef survey began in 2010 on the central West Florida Shelf, with sampling from May through October [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 5]. It was expanded to the Florida Panhandle in 2014 and to the remaining Florida Gulf coast waters in 2016, adding a deep stratum of 110–180 m [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. A complementary artificial reef survey began in 2014 because artificial reefs receive disproportionately high fishing effort relative to natural reefs [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6].

Table 1 of Switzer et al. (2023) compares the three surveys [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 4]. The FWRI survey differs from the other two in four respects:

- Random sampling in a two-stage design, where SEAMAP samples in a targeted way and Panama City samples in a targeted or random way.
- Habitat mapping with side scan sonar, where SEAMAP uses multibeam sonar.
- Cut Atlantic Mackerel *Scomber scombrus* as bait, where SEAMAP uses squid.
- Enumeration of all fishes, where SEAMAP enumerates managed fishes only.

### Habitat mapping

Habitat data for natural reefs in the eastern Gulf were of insufficient spatial resolution to direct sampling, so standardised mapping surveys were run at randomly selected sites [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 5]. Each mapping survey covered the selected sampling unit plus 20 surrounding units, a mapped area of 2.1 km², using an L3-Klein 3900 side-scan sonar at 445 kHz [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 5]. Reef features more than 2 m across were digitised as polygons and classified with a derivative of the geoform and surface geological components of the Coastal and Marine Ecological Classification Standard [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 5]. The program reports about 8,000 km² mapped, described on the slide as the largest mapping effort in the Gulf [FWRI 2023, slide 17].

Table 2 of Switzer et al. (2023) ranks habitat classes by relief [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 5]:

- **Natural, low relief:** seagrass, flat hard bottom, pavement, and mixed hard bottom.
- **Natural, moderate relief:** potholes, rubble field, and fracture.
- **Natural, high relief:** reef rubble, fragmented hard bottom, patch reefs, aggregate coral reef, ledge, boulder, spur and groove, spring or sink, pinnacle, and escarpment.
- **Artificial, low relief:** cable, dredge deposit, tires, dredged channel, rock piles, and pipelines.
- **Artificial, moderate relief:** construction materials and reef modules.
- **Artificial, high relief:** chicken coops, marine wreckage, vehicles, vessels, military tanks, oil-platform materials, and aircraft.

Because the maps came from side-scan sonar, a quantitative determination of reef-specific relief was impractical [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 16].

### Sampling frame

The original FWRI design allocated effort between two latitudinal strata (26°N–27°N and above 27°N–28°N) and two depth strata (nearshore, 9–37 m; offshore, above 37–110 m) [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 5]. Effort was equal between latitudinal strata and proportional to area among depth strata, and sampling units measured 0.54 km north to south by 0.18 km east to west [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 5]. In the second stage, a sampling unit containing natural reef was selected at random and one or two reef features at least 100 m apart were sampled [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 5–6].

The artificial reef frame assumed that the universe was fully described, because all legally deployed artificial reefs must be reported to the state of Florida [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. Point locations of known shipwrecks and artificial reefs were intersected with the sampling frame to identify units that contained artificial reef, and one feature was selected in the second stage [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. In the revised design, the primary sampling unit for artificial reefs is 0.18 × 0.18 km, produced by dividing each original 0.54 × 0.18 km unit into three identical grids [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 8].

### Strata

Classification and regression tree analyses, run independently three times, delineated spatial strata, natural reef habitat strata, and artificial reef habitat strata [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 7]. The spatial analysis used all three surveys (N = 3487, natural reefs only), and the habitat analyses used FWRI data only (natural N = 2005, artificial N = 260), because the random mapping approach made those data representative of the habitats available [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 7].

The resulting spatial scheme is a 3 × 3 grid of regions and depths [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 10]:

- **Regions:** north-central Gulf, Big Bend, and south Florida.
- **Depths:** nearshore (10–25 m), offshore (above 25–50 m), and deep (above 50–180 m).

Assemblage breaks were evident at about 83°W, which isolated shallow assemblages off southwestern Florida, and at about 50 m [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 10]. Panhandle assemblages differed from those on the West Florida Shelf, with breaks at 84.9°W and 84.5°W for the 10% and 20% minimum group sizes [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 10].

Habitat strata combine relief and feature size [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 10–11]:

- **Natural reefs:** three relief classes (low, moderate, high) and three size classes (below 100 m², 100–1000 m², above 1000 m²). Assemblages on habitat with a relief rank of 3 or lower (flat hard bottom, pavement, mixed hard bottom) differed from all other habitats, and areal breaks ranged from 60 to 12,900 m².
- **Artificial reefs:** three relief classes and three size classes (below 25 m², 25–100 m², above 100 m²). Areal breaks ranged from 21 to 1350 m², and low-relief artificial habitats (tires, rock piles, pipelines, and to some extent construction materials) differed from higher-relief habitats.

The natural and artificial reef features identified span nearly four orders of magnitude in size [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 17]. The authors justify the depth strata by fishery: the nearshore stratum includes many habitats targeted by the recreational fishery, much of the commercial vertical-line fishery operates in the offshore stratum, and the commercial longline fishery operates primarily in the deep stratum [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 15–16]. The nearshore stratum also isolates reef assemblages likely connected to estuarine habitats, especially for species that use estuarine nursery grounds, such as Gag and Gray Snapper [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 15].

### Effort allocation

Allocation weights each stratum by habitat availability and managed-species richness. The rule is O<sub>NATALLOC</sub> = (H<sub>NATSTRAT</sub>)<sup>1/4</sup> × R<sub>NATSTRAT</sub> (equation transcribed from the typeset page), where the fourth root of extrapolated habitat availability "downweight[s] several highly expansive habitat strata and ensure[s] equal contribution of habitat availability and managed species richness to final allocation" [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 8]. The same form applies to artificial reefs [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 8]. Richness acts as a proxy for variance in allocation [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 18].

At 1000 natural reef and 200 artificial reef deployments per year, effort within a spatial stratum ranged from 77 sites (south Florida deep) to 159 sites (Big Bend offshore) for natural reefs, and from 10 sites (south Florida deep) to 51 sites (north-central Gulf offshore) for artificial reefs [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 11–12]. Every stratum receives at least two sites per year [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 11]. Low-relief, large-scale habitat, and to a lesser degree high-relief, large-scale habitat, made up most available natural reef in all spatial strata, with especially high availability in the Big Bend nearshore stratum [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 11]. Most artificial reefs occurred in the north-central Gulf and, to a lesser extent, the Big Bend nearshore zone [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 11].

### Simulated performance

Switzer et al. (2023) simulated six federally managed species (Gray Triggerfish, Red Grouper, Red Snapper, Gag, Vermilion Snapper, and Greater Amberjack *Seriola dumerili*) separately for natural and artificial reefs, chosen because they differ in distribution, habitat occupancy, and site-specific abundance [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 8]. The acceptable thresholds were relative median bias (RBIAS) below 15% in absolute value and relative median absolute deviation (RMAD) below 30% [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 10].

**Natural reefs.** The optimal design gave weighted RBIAS below 10% in absolute value and weighted RMAD below 30% for most species [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 13]. Greater Amberjack had the highest weighted RBIAS (+55%) and RMAD (58%), and both rose with increasing stratification from simple random to spatially stratified to optimal [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 13]. The authors attribute this to exceptionally high variability in one or two strata that cover a large part of the study area and were influential in weighted analyses [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 19]. Red Snapper had relative median bias below 5% [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 15]. Among scenarios, weighted RBIAS was lowest for simple random allocation (−5% to 7%), and weighted RMAD ranged 16%–39% for simple random, 10%–38% for spatially stratified, and 15%–58% for optimal allocation [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 13]. Unweighted RBIAS ranged from −35% to +15% for simple random, −20% to +10% for spatially stratified, and +2% to +12% for optimal; unweighted RMAD for Vermilion Snapper fell from 35% (simple random) to 22% (spatially stratified) to 15% (optimal) [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 13].

**Artificial reefs.** Weighted RBIAS ranged from −17% to +35% across scenarios and species, and weighted RMAD from 20% to 47% [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 13–14]. Under the optimal design, Gray Triggerfish, Red Snapper, and Greater Amberjack had weighted RBIAS below 10% in absolute value, and Gray Triggerfish and Greater Amberjack had weighted RMAD below 20% [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 13]. Weighted RMAD was highest for Red Snapper (40% or more), Gag (33% or more), and Red Grouper (40% or more) [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 14]. The authors state that performance "was poorest for species with relatively high proportion of zero counts (e.g., Red Grouper, Gag)" [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 19].

Reuse of the figures above carries these caveats:

- The abstract names the third region "southwest Florida", whereas the Results and Figure 3 name it "south Florida" [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 1, 10].
- Three of 81 natural reef sampling strata were excluded because some spatial strata lacked identified habitat strata, and the simulations used 70 natural reef strata, so the 81 and 70 refer to different steps [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 9, 11].
- The statement that unweighted estimates had biases below 10% and imprecision below 30% for most species [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 18] does not hold for artificial reefs, where unweighted RBIAS ranged from −18% to +40% and RMAD from 20% to 53% [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 14].

The authors conclude that there were no indications of large-scale systematic bias that would preclude implementation, and that performance will be reevaluated when more data are available, about five years on [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 19].

### Nursery counterpart

The juvenile counterpart to the offshore survey is the West Florida Shelf Inshore (WI) survey, begun in 2008. The standard long-term estuarine survey "undersampled a preferred juvenile habitat: polyhaline (salinity >18) seagrass beds", which left indices of juvenile estuarine-dependent reef fish highly variable [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9]. The WI survey supplements the standard survey in Apalachicola Bay, Tampa Bay, and Charlotte Harbor, and extends to St. Andrew Bay and the Big Bend [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9]. Its universe is defined by 185 × 185 m cells containing polyhaline seagrass, stratified by space with randomly selected sites, and covers about 224 km² in the standard estuaries and about 221 km² in the adjacent estuaries [Schrandt et al. 2024, pp. 9–10].

The survey uses the 6.1-m otter trawl, which must sample a path with at least 50% submerged aquatic vegetation cover and is towed for half the standard distance (0.1 nautical mile at 1.2 knots, about 5 minutes) [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 10]. A gear comparison and power simulation for seven species (Schrandt et al. 2021b, as reported in Schrandt et al. 2024) found the trawl more efficient than the haul seine for many reef species, and haul seine sampling ended in 2019 while trawl sample size increased [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 11]. The trawl abundance estimates for seven reef fish species were greater in the WI survey than in the long-term survey [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 12]. The offshore and nursery surveys connect through species such as Gag and Gray Snapper, which Switzer et al. (2023) describe as using estuarine nursery grounds and which Schrandt et al. (2024) report at higher densities on seagrass shoals [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 15; Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 11].

## Methods and approaches

### Strata delineation

For each of the three analyses, a site-by-abundance matrix of maximum count per deployment (MaxN) was built for all reef-associated taxa with fishery management plans for Gulf populations (31 taxa, Table 3) [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 7]. Abundances were fourth-root transformed to reduce the influence of abundant taxa [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 7]. Bray–Curtis similarity matrices included a dummy variable with value 1 for all samples, so that sites with no species observed were treated as similar [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 7].

Each analysis used the LINKTREE algorithm in PRIMER 7, "a constrained binary divisive clustering approach in which subdivisions of the environmental data must have some explanatory ability", with SIMPROF tests (α = 0.05) for significance between groups [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 7]. Minimum group sizes of 10%, 15%, and 20% of available data were tried, each with 9999 permutations [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 7]. The analyses were restricted to 2014–2017 and to explanatory variables that could be identified a priori; for spatial strata the candidates were latitude, longitude, and depth [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 7].

### Habitat availability

Habitat availability came from the FWRI natural reef mapping data because they followed standardised, randomised protocols and so represent unmapped areas [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 8]. Habitat coverage within a spatial stratum is the proportion of mapped area in each habitat class (PNATHAB = ANATHAB / AMAPPED), multiplied by the spatial stratum area (HNATTRAT = ASPATSTRAT × PNATHAB) to extrapolate availability [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 8]. Artificial reef intersections were made with replacement, so one primary sampling unit can fall under several habitat strata, including natural reef [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 8]. Where managed-species richness was unavailable for a stratum because no historical S-BRUV data existed (9% of natural reef strata), a minimum richness of 1 was assigned so that effort still occurred there [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 8].

### Field and video protocol

Sampling occurs in daylight, from 1 hour after sunrise to 1 hour before sunset [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. The FWRI array holds two stereo video units facing 180° apart, whereas the SEAMAP and Panama City arrays hold four units at 90° [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. Cameras are Point Gray Blackfly units with Kowa lenses, recording 1920 × 1200 pixels with an 86.3° horizontal field of view, mounted 30–50 cm above the bottom [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. Arrays are baited with 0.45 kg of previously frozen cut Atlantic Mackerel (FWRI and Panama City) or squid (SEAMAP) and soaked for about 30 minutes so that the sediment plume dissipates [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. One stereo unit with a clear view of reef habitat is selected at random, and 20 minutes of video are annotated after the sediment cloud clears; videos with low visibility, large obstructions, or camera malfunction are not processed [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. Abundance is MaxN, the maximum number of a species in view at any time in the 20 minutes [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. Fork lengths of managed fishes are measured in SeaGIS software at the time of MaxN or when most measurements are possible; observed fish span about 50–1500 mm, and about 95% span 100–700 mm [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. The authors note that they have not tested whether bait type influences the abundance observed, and that minor habitat coding discrepancies exist among the three surveys [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 6–7].

Water quality (temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen) is recorded at each site [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 6]. Acoustic data are also collected at a subset of S-BRUV sites, several hundred in 2023 [FWRI 2023, slide 20].

### Count standardisation

Simulation inputs were MaxN counts for each species from 2014–2017 deployments on natural and artificial reefs [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 9]. Counts were modelled with Bayesian Poisson and negative binomial regression, both with a natural log link, with sample year and a categorical sampling-stratum variable (70 levels for natural reefs, 44 for artificial reefs) as terms, and with stratum and year as random effects on the intercept [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 9]. In the notation of the paper, log(μ_ijk) = β0(SamplingStrata_j) + β1(Year_k). Zeros stayed in the data. Overdispersion and zero inflation were tested with the testDispersion and testZeroInflation functions of the R package DHARMa, and a negative binomial model replaced the Poisson model if either was found [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 9].

Models were fitted in R 4.0.3 with rstanarm, using three Hamiltonian Monte Carlo chains of 10,000 iterations with 5000 discarded as warm-up [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 9]. Priors were weakly informative: normal (mean 0, standard deviation 2.5) for hyper-means and exponential (scale 1) for standard deviations and the overdispersion parameter [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 9]. Diagnostics were Gelman–Rubin potential scale-reduction factors, posterior predictive checks in shinystan, and QQ plots from the DHARMa testResiduals function [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 9–10].

Observed MaxN per deployment shows why the distribution varied by species. Table 4 reports, for 2014–2017, a mean MaxN of 0.38 for Red Grouper on natural reefs with 72% zeros, against 4.67 for Red Snapper on artificial reefs with 40% zeros, and 0.08 for Gag on natural reefs with 95% zeros [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 9]. Most species were overdispersed on both habitats, so negative binomial models were used; Red Grouper was the sole exception, and a Poisson model fit both its natural and artificial reef data [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 12]. The negative binomial model for Vermilion Snapper on artificial reefs showed poor chain mixing and nonconvergence even after 100,000 iterations with a 50,000-iteration warm-up, so that species was omitted from the artificial reef analysis [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 12–13]. Strata with no historical data were left out of the standardisation, because "analyses included only sampling strata for which data were available" [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 9].

### Simulation procedure

The simulation compared these allocation scenarios: the optimal allocation, a spatially stratified scenario similar to the earlier FWRI design, and simple random allocation [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 9]. In each, the total was fixed at 1000 samples for natural reefs and 200 for artificial reefs and the process was repeated 1000 times [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 10]. Performance was measured by RBIAS = med(μ_estimated − μ_true) / μ_true and RMAD = med(|μ_estimated − μ_true|) / μ_true, using medians because sampling distributions were skewed [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 10]. The 30% RMAD threshold corresponds to the coefficient of variation threshold often used to evaluate indices for stock assessment [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 10]. Estimates were produced with and without area weighting: area weighting is generally preferred for representative indices, and the unweighted runs quantify bias if analyses do not account for the area sampled in each stratum [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 10]. The authors state that the thresholds "may not be appropriate for all studies" [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 10].

The interpretation of the simulations depends on each analysis having the correct species-specific distribution, and some strata had only a handful of observations from which to simulate, while others were excluded for lack of data [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 19].

## Open questions

- Reef-specific relief could not be quantified from side-scan sonar, and vertical relief may also reduce detection probability in visual surveys [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 16]. How relief should enter the strata when it is measured directly remains open.
- For species with many zero counts, such as Red Grouper and Gag, the optimal design performed poorest on artificial reefs [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 19]. The authors suggest that a multiframe sampling design could reduce bias and imprecision further [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 19].
- Habitat strata and allocation are tuned to a species set. The authors point to spatiotemporal models that optimise both stratification and allocation for multiple species at once as promising [Switzer et al. 2023, p. 18].
- The western Gulf has randomised habitat mapping gaps. Mapping there is largely multibeam data collected near well-known reef features, not under a randomised design, so it cannot give unbiased habitat availability, and the western Gulf design is to be revised iteratively [Switzer et al. 2023, pp. 19–20].
- The workshop slides list a research priority to identify variation in reef fish abundance or assemblage structure in relation to forage fish abundance from underwater video, and another on inshore and offshore connectivity [FWRI 2023, slides 29–30]. Neither is resolved in the sources reviewed.
