# FIM survey design

## Summary

The Fisheries-Independent Monitoring (FIM) program of the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission, Fish and Wildlife Research Institute, runs a standardised, multispecies, stratified-random estuarine survey with three core gears: a 21.3-m center-bag seine, a 183-m haul seine, and a 6.1-m otter trawl [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 3]. Monitoring began in Tampa Bay and Charlotte Harbor in 1989 and now covers seven sampling units on the Gulf and Atlantic coasts [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 3]. The 1996 design (monthly, daytime, stratified-random) has been kept through geographic expansion, and later modifications were added to it as new strata, new gears in new areas, or seasonal subsets, with a reduction following each expansion [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 2]. Analysts who use FIM data must decide how to treat these design changes. Published guidance is to include zone as a factor, truncate or split the index, or restrict the series to months sampled throughout [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 19]. This article describes the design, the changes and their statistical consequences, the data collected, access routes, and the filters that five analyses applied.

## Key points

### Objectives

The program was planned from 1985 after suspected declines of red drum, *Sciaenops ocellatus*, and was first intended to focus on juvenile red drum. Early leaders instead designed it to assess multispecies population trends across habitats and life-history stages [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 2]. Current workshop material states that the program is designed to support single-species assessment, to support multispecies, ecosystem-based modelling, and to address emerging issues, and that this "requires a multi-species, multi-gear, multi-habitat approach" [FWRI 2023, slide 5]. The design is therefore not tuned to one metric: "the design is not optimized around a specific metric" [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4].

The primary objective is to monitor annual, not monthly, trends, so statistical power is greater for annual than for monthly comparisons [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]. Dedicated funding began with a $400,000 legislative appropriation in 1986 and a $260,000 federal Sportfish Restoration grant in 1987. Since 1995 the program has relied on saltwater fishing licence sales and competitive grants, and grants supply about half of its funding [Schrandt et al. 2024, pp. 2–3].

### Start years

Monitoring started in Tampa Bay and Charlotte Harbor in 1989, the northern Indian River Lagoon in 1990, Cedar Key in 1996, the southern Indian River Lagoon in 1997, Apalachicola Bay in 1998, and Northeast Florida in 2001 [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 3]. The workshop timeline gives the same years and adds St. Andrew Bay and the Big Bend in 2008, South Atlantic offshore sampling in 2012, juvenile snook habitat in 2015, and Choctawhatchee Bay in 2021 [FWRI 2023, slide 7]. Short-term studies fill gaps outside the long-term estuaries, for example Pensacola Bay and St. Joseph Bay in 2017–2019 and Sarasota Bay in 2009 [FWRI 2023, slide 4]. Field laboratories operate at Jacksonville, Eastpoint, Cedar Key, St. Petersburg, Melbourne, and Charlotte Harbor [FWRI 2023, slide 3].

Effective series length differs from the estuary start year because analyses use gear-specific records. Whaley et al. (2023) used Apalachicola Bay data from 2001, although the estuary began in 1998, because their large-juvenile haul seine series starts in August 2001 and their small-juvenile seine series in June 2003 [Whaley et al. 2023, p. 5]. Purtlebaugh et al. (2020) used 1997–2018 for the Suwannee River estuary [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4].

### Standard gears

The standard design uses the three gears below. Dimensions below are from Schrandt et al. (2024) unless stated.

| Gear | Dimensions and mesh | Depth limit | Area per set | Typical targets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Center-bag seine | 21.3 m x 2.0 m, 3.2-mm mesh | 1.8 m | about 140 m² (bay), about 68 m² (river) | Age-0 and small juveniles, for example snook under 100 mm standard length (SL) |
| Haul seine | 183 m x 3.0 m, 38-mm mesh | 2.5 m or less | about 4120 m² (bay) | Larger fish, for example snook over 300 mm SL; subadults and adults |
| Otter trawl | 6.1 m, 3.2-mm mesh bag liner | 1.0–7.6 m | about 1440 m² (bay), about 720 m² (river) | Age-0 and juveniles from deeper water |

Sources for the table: dimensions, mesh, depths and areas [Schrandt et al. 2024, pp. 3–4]; snook size targets [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]; trawl and seine targets [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4].

Purtlebaugh et al. (2020) describe the 21.3-m seine and the trawl as targeting "primarily age-0 and juvenile fishes from different depths", with the haul seine targeting "subadult and adult fish along shorelines" [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. The workshop timeline labels the 21.3-m seine "juveniles" and the 183-m seine "adults" [FWRI 2023, slide 7]. Haul seines are set by boat in a rectangular shape along shorelines and pulled together at the water's edge [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 10]. A standard center-bag seine haul pulls the net 9.1 m with a 15.5-m line [Faletti et al. 2019, p. 3]. The sampling universe runs from the estuary mouth through the tidally influenced portion of major rivers [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]. All fish and macroinvertebrates are identified and counted [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 10].

Where sources disagree on gear details, the disagreement is small but affects reproducibility:

- Haul seine height is 3.0 m in Schrandt et al. 2024 [p. 3] and 2.5 m in Whaley et al. 2023 [p. 5] and in Table 1 of Purtlebaugh et al. 2020 [p. 6].
- Trawl depth range is 1.0–7.6 m in Schrandt et al. 2024 [p. 4] and 1.8–7.6 m in Purtlebaugh et al. 2020 [p. 4].
- Seine depth is 1.8 m in rivers and 1.5 m in open estuary in Whaley et al. 2023 [p. 5] and Purtlebaugh et al. 2020 [p. 4], against a single 1.8-m limit in Schrandt et al. 2024 [p. 3].

### Stratified-random design

Strata are defined by space and habitat. Space strata are approximately homogeneous biological and water-quality "zones" within an estuary. Habitat strata are presence or absence of submerged aquatic vegetation (SAV) and presence or absence of overhanging shoreline vegetation such as mangroves, because "Early data analyses suggested species assemblages differed among these habitat strata" [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]. Addis (2020) states strata by gear as depth, shore type, and bottom vegetation [Addis 2020, p. 18]. The workshop slide lists "Natural or artificial reefs" among its example habitat strata [FWRI 2023, slide 10].

Selection is cell-based. "The sampling universe within each estuary is divided into cells (185 m × 185 m), with each cell assigned binary values for whether each combination of gear and strata is likely to be available for sampling", and "Cells are then selected in a stratified random manner, without replacement, for sampling each month" [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]. Monthly effort per stratum is proportional to the number of potential sites but weighted toward vegetated habitats, and total monthly effort depends on funding [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]. Sampling is daytime only [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4].

Analysis papers describe the grid differently. Whaley et al. (2023) and Purtlebaugh et al. (2020) describe cells of 1 minute latitude by 1 minute longitude within zones [Whaley et al. 2023, pp. 3–4; Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. Faletti et al. (2019) and Nehemiah et al. (2022) describe 1 nautical mile grids, and Faletti et al. add a 183 m² microgrid chosen at random as the starting point for the habitat search [Faletti et al. 2019, p. 3; Nehemiah et al. 2022, p. 3]. The raw files do not reconcile these descriptions with the 185 m cell. Effort per gear and habitat is roughly proportional to available habitat [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4]. Apalachicola Bay received on average 64 samples per month [Whaley et al. 2023, p. 3]. Suwannee estuary effort is tabulated by year, for example 192 haul seine hauls per year in 1999–2018 and 120 bay trawls per year in 2002–2018 [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 6].

### Design changes

All three case studies in Schrandt et al. (2024) began as expansions and were followed by reductions or streamlining [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 2]. Expansions were designed to cost 50–70% less than a new laboratory by using existing laboratories, gears, and protocols [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 3].

**Juvenile snook survey.** Stock assessment analysts needed abundance, survival, and growth for juvenile snook of 100–300 mm SL, which were poorly represented in either seine, especially in Charlotte Harbor and the southern Indian River Lagoon [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4]. Gear testing ran from July 2014 to June 2015 in Tampa Bay, Charlotte Harbor, and the Indian River Lagoon, with 1,872 seine samples and 2,315 juvenile snook [Schrandt et al. 2024, pp. 5–6]. The 21.3-m seine took more than 95% of the catch against an experimental 40-m seine with 25-mm mesh, and collected the age-0 fish that the 40-m seine largely missed [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 6]. In January 2016 the 21.3-m seine was allocated additional monthly effort in backwaters and tidal tributaries, treated as a new geographic stratum: 8 hauls per month in Tampa Bay, 30 in Charlotte Harbor across 27 tidal creeks, and 24 in the Indian River Lagoon, including the St. Lucie and Loxahatchee rivers that the standard design did not cover [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 7]. These sum to 62 hauls per month, which is consistent with the stated annual sample size of 744 [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9]. A reduction in 2021 followed after five years of level funding: of four scenarios of 6 to 9 months, the 9-month scenario (no sampling in March, May, or July) cut effort by "25%, 333 annual net hauls" [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9]. The paper does not state the base to which 25% applies; 333 divided by 0.25 gives 1,332 (derived, not stated), which does not match the 744 hauls of the snook survey.

**Polyhaline seagrass trawl survey.** The standard survey undersampled polyhaline (salinity above 18) seagrass beds, a preferred juvenile reef fish habitat, and indices for juvenile estuarine-dependent reef fishes were highly variable [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9]. The West Florida Shelf Inshore (WI) survey began in 2008 in Apalachicola Bay, Tampa Bay, and Charlotte Harbor, plus St. Andrew Bay and the Big Bend, covering about 224 km² in standard estuaries (5–10% of the standard universe) and about 221 km² in the new estuaries [Schrandt et al. 2024, pp. 9–10]. Its trawl must run through at least 50% SAV cover and is towed half the standard distance (0.1 against 0.2 nautical miles) [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 10]. Sampling months were narrowed from May–November to June–November and unproductive areas dropped (Keaton Beach after 2008, St. Marks in 2019) [Schrandt et al. 2024, pp. 10–11]. Haul seine sampling ended in all WI estuaries in 2019 and trawl effort increased, after a power analysis found the trawl more efficient for many reef species [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 11]. Annual WI trawl hauls across the five estuaries were 534 in 2008, 434 in a typical year of 2009–2015, 372 in 2016–2017, 612 in 2019, and 270 in 2020 because COVID-19 reduced sampling [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 11, Table 1, read from the printed table].

**Western Panhandle and seatrout.** In 2020 FWC defined five spotted seatrout management regions that matched genetic breaks, and the Western Panhandle had no standard FIM survey [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 13]. Less than 20% of the annual cost of a traditional single-estuary survey was available [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 13]. Monthly sampling began in June 2020 with 6.1-m trawls in Choctawhatchee Bay, Santa Rosa Sound, and St. Andrew Bay (through the WI survey) and 183-m haul seines in Choctawhatchee Bay and Santa Rosa Sound only [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 15]. The trawl replaced the 21.3-m seine for age-0 fish, which gave an immediate 13-year (2008–2020) series for St. Andrew Bay [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 15]. The design samples June–November (trawls) and June–December (haul seines) and addresses only some regional data needs [Schrandt et al. 2024, pp. 15–17]. Funding of about 33% of the cost of a field laboratory was met by a $150k reallocation and by halving standard FIM trawling, with some loss of blue crab, *Callinectes sapidus*, and pink shrimp, *Farfantepenaeus duorarum*, data [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 17].

**Source disagreements.**

- Schrandt et al. 2024 say six estuaries in the Figure 1 caption and seven in the text, because the Indian River Lagoon is one system sampled as two units [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 3]. Analyses cite six estuaries [Nehemiah et al. 2022, p. 4].
- Pre-fishery spotted seatrout are 100–325 mm SL in the text and 100–326 mm SL in the Figure 11 caption of Schrandt et al. 2024 [pp. 15–16]; the fishery class begins at 326 mm.
- The workshop timeline places 6.1-m trawls at 2005 and juvenile snook habitat at 2015 [FWRI 2023, slide 7], whereas Schrandt et al. 2024 give 1996 for the finalised gear set and January 2016 for the snook modification [pp. 2, 7].
- The 25% reduction of 333 hauls has no stated base (see above).

### Standardising across changes

Schrandt et al. 2024 state the assumption on which indices rest: "changes in relative abundance through time represent actual changes in the stock being assessed, and not changes in survey design" [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 19]. They recommend four responses.

- Standardise with generalised linear or additive models "that can adjust for resultant changes while retaining the maximum amount of data possible" [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 19].
- When samples are added in a new zone within a sampled estuary, include zone as a factor. When samples are added within an existing zone, the extra sample size reduces variance [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 19].
- When designs diverge too far for continuity, truncate the series or build a split index [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 19].
- When monthly sampling becomes bimonthly, remove the dropped months from the earlier data to yield one series of commonly sampled months [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 19].

Length composition needs separate attention. If sampling no longer covers peak juvenile recruitment, or a gear change alters size selectivity, the assessment model "would need to be adjusted" [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 19]. Authors also advise open dialogue between data collectors and analysts and exploration of the data before choosing an adjustment [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 19]. Zone handling in practice: Nehemiah et al. (2022) kept sampling zone as a candidate covariate and dropped Zone F for low sample size and limited gears [Nehemiah et al. 2022, p. 4]; Addis (2020) removed "months, zones, or strata that rarely encountered Red Drum" before standardising [Addis 2020, p. 18].

### Data beyond catch

At each sample the program records temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and pH, as integrated values and profiles [FWRI 2023, slide 15], and microhabitat: substrate cover and composition, biota cover and composition, inundation or vegetative cover, and substrate relief [FWRI 2023, slide 16]. Faletti et al. (2019) used a YSI handheld multiparameter meter and visually estimated SAV cover in ten percent increments [Faletti et al. 2019, p. 3]. Nehemiah et al. (2022) built salinity from surface, bottom, and 1-m interval readings at 21.3-m seine sites [Nehemiah et al. 2022, p. 4]. Life-history data include age and growth, reproduction, and mercury [FWRI 2023, slide 18]; Addis (2020) lists length, weight, sex, maturity, age, and diet [Addis 2020, p. 18]. More than 40,000 stomach samples had been processed by 2023 [FWRI 2023, slide 19].

For offshore reef fish the program mapped about 8,000 km² of natural and artificial reef with side scan sonar, which directs reef fish sampling effort, and added stereo cameras and hooked gear [FWRI 2023, slides 12 and 17]. The offshore component began in 2008 [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 3]. Offshore design is covered in [[reef-fish-survey-design]].

### Data access

Inshore FIM data are held in SQL Server; SAS and CSV exports are possible but require more processing time, and the program was migrating all data to SQL databases in 2023 [FWRI 2023, slide 26]. Metadata document tables, columns, relationships, effort calculations, length extrapolations, and code lists, with methods in procedure manuals [FWRI 2023, slide 27]. Planned access routes include a repository, a web interface, and an R package for querying FIM data [FWRI 2023, slide 42]. No portal address is given on the slides [FWRI 2023, slide 42]. Current practice in the papers is access on request: "All Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission (FWC) data and records fall under the Sunshine Law", so data are available to the public on request to the program [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 1]. Faletti et al. (2019) direct requests to the FWRI data curator [Faletti et al. 2019, p. 1]. Whaley et al. (2023) deposited their analysis data in the FWC library repository [Whaley et al. 2023, p. 1]. Annual data summary reports describe site selection and gear procedures, for example the 2016 summary, FWC Report 727 [Nehemiah et al. 2022, p. 10].

| Purtlebaugh et al. 2020 [pp. 4–6] | Common snook; Suwannee estuary | All gears for annual counts; haul seine only for occurrence | None | 1997–2018 | More than 97% of snook were in the haul seine; effort constant from 2002 |
| Nehemiah et al. 2022 [pp. 3–4] | Spotted seatrout; Cedar Key | Juvenile: 21.3-m seine; adult: 183-m haul seine | Juvenile under 100 mm SL; adult over 250 mm SL | Juvenile peak June–November; adult 1997–2018, year-round | Zones B and C; Zone F excluded; salinity only from sites with juveniles |

Addis (2020) states the young-of-the-year limit as "smaller than or equal to 40 mm" in one place and "smaller than 40 mm SL" in another [Addis 2020, p. 18].

## Methods and approaches

Procedures a lab member would reuse for FIM data:

1. Select the gear that samples the life stage. Use the 21.3-m seine for age-0 fish (under 100 mm SL), the 183-m haul seine for subadults and adults, and the trawl for deeper habitat [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 4; Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, p. 4].
2. Select months from recruitment. Whaley et al. (2023) set peak seasons from monthly length-frequency histograms [Whaley et al. 2023, p. 5]. Nehemiah et al. (2022) defined peak months as those with the most juveniles caught [Nehemiah et al. 2022, p. 4].
3. Check effort history. Purtlebaugh et al. (2020) combined gears because effort was nearly constant in 1997–2018 and changes preceded the first snook, then used only the haul seine for occurrence [Purtlebaugh et al. 2020, pp. 4–6]. Tabulate hauls per gear, zone, and year before choosing the subset.
4. Standardise with a delta model. Nehemiah et al. (2022) fitted a log-normal model to positive catches and a binomial model to presence, with covariates year, month, bottom and shoreline vegetation, bottom type, temperature, salinity, and zone, chosen by forward stepwise regression. The index is the product of the two least-squares means, with uncertainty from 10,000 Monte Carlo iterations [Nehemiah et al. 2022, p. 4]. Addis (2020) used a gamma positive model with log link [Addis 2020, p. 18].
5. Model zeros explicitly. Zero hauls enter the binomial component [Addis 2020, p. 18]. Faletti et al. (2019) compared four zero-inflated and zero-altered families by Akaike information criterion and chose a zero-altered negative binomial model, fitted separately for each estuary and run with the MASS and car packages in R [Faletti et al. 2019, pp. 5–6].
6. Report density in comparable units, for example fish per 100 m² for seines [Faletti et al. 2019, p. 3; Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 8].
7. After a design change, apply the options in "Standardising across changes" and examine data before and after the change.

## Open questions

- Which base the 25% reduction of the snook survey refers to, and how the reduced snook series compares with the pre-2021 series [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 9].
- Whether the Western Panhandle design, lacking a full gear suite and year-round sampling, supports management of species other than spotted seatrout; the authors hope a full FIM survey can be established there [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 17].
- How decades of data affect power. The review notes that "decades of data may be needed to reliably detect trends in abundance" for some species [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 18].
- How to sustain database management. The authors recommend about 25% of grant budgets and note that the program has no dedicated database manager [Schrandt et al. 2024, p. 20].
- Whether the planned repository and R package will provide public query access [FWRI 2023, slide 42].
